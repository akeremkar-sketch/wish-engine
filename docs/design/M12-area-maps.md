# M12: area maps and research

The output of ten read-only agents, 18 November 2026 (eight mapped one area each from the code, two researched the web); the backlog behind [M12-road-to-1-0.md](M12-road-to-1-0.md). Written by agents and not checked line by line: verify a claim against the code before building on it.
Sizes S < M < L < XL as in docs/ROADMAP.md. need: must / should / later = the mapping agent's view of what 1.0 needs.

## Area: Graphics quality (key graphics) — score now 3, plausible after 5.5
Score after assumes: About 5 with the eight 'must' gaps closed (pass structure and settings, post stack A with FXAA, sky-driven ambient plus environment maps, cascaded shadows with masked casters, material assets with textures on primitives, blended transparency, direct presentation with tick interpolation, render statistics). About 6 if the 'should' group is also done (culling and instancing, MSAA, ambient occlusion, spot shadows, GPU skinning, HDR particles and wider glTF coverage) and the showcase game is re-lit with the new defaults. Not higher before 1.0: Godot's 6 and Unity's 8 include global illumination, reflection probes, volumetrics and mature material tooling, which stay in the 'later' group. The M12 proposal's estimate of 'about 6' from G1-G3 alone looks optimistic to me, because G1-G3 leave out transparency, textures on primitives, presentation and scale.

### Gaps (ordered by the mapper: most score for least work first)
1. [M | must] Pass structure, pooled targets and a schema'd RenderSettings: split Renderer::render into named passes with declared inputs and outputs, keep HDR/depth/shadow targets and uniform buffers across frames (keyed by size), and add one settings resource (plus a per-camera override) that every later effect reads.
   - Why: Every item below needs intermediate targets and an on/off switch. Without it each effect is bolted into a 400-line function, and per-frame creation of a 2048x2048 shadow texture and all buffers is wasted time on every frame a player sees.
   - Depends on: Nothing. Resolve the ADR-012 versus ROADMAP M11 contradiction in docs/DECISIONS.md first (working method rule 4).
   - Risk: Low. Pure refactor: existing goldens must stay byte-comparable, which is the test. Settings must be components/resources in wish_types (schema first, compiles on wasm32); scene files omit defaults, so the chosen defaults define the look of every existing scene.
2. [M | must] Post stack A: HDR bloom, distance and height fog, colour grading (contrast, saturation, white balance) and vignette, FXAA; move particles before tone mapping so they can glow.
   - Why: Largest visible change per unit of work: emissive is already in physical units, so bloom pays off at once; fog gives depth to the flat open scenes in the showcase game; the image today has hard jaggies on every edge.
   - Depends on: Pass structure and RenderSettings.
   - Risk: Sampler bilinear precision differs between WARP and lavapipe; a blur chain can push more than 0.5% of pixels past 2/255. Mitigation: do bloom taps with textureLoad and weights computed in the shader, re-run the existing two-adapter measurement, one golden plus an on/off comparison test per effect. All new shaders must guard denominators (WARP lost the device on a flattened divide by zero). Changes every existing golden and the examples' scenario goldens once.
3. [M | must] Sky as the light source, step 1: ambient taken from the sky (hemisphere or SH9 of the gradient, computed on the CPU) instead of one flat colour, roughness-aware sky reflection, a sun disc tied to the DirectionalLight. Step 2: HDR environment maps (equirect .hdr -> irradiance SH + prefiltered specular cube, built on the CPU and cached under .wish/), drawn as the skybox.
   - Why: Flat ambient is why every scene looks like untextured plastic: undersides and shadowed sides have no shape, metals reflect a blur-free gradient. IBL is the difference between 'programmer art' and 'lit'. ADR-012 names it in the 1.0 baseline.
   - Depends on: Step 1: nothing (S on its own). Step 2: pass structure; an asset kind for environments (wish_asset meta, asset_info, editor parity row); cube textures (WebGPU core).
   - Risk: Prefiltering on the GPU would differ per driver; do it on the CPU like the mip chain so goldens hold. The .hdr decoder is hostile input: inside `guarded`, with pixel budgets. Real HDRIs are large downloads with mixed licences: ship none in the repository, generate the test environment in code.
4. [L | must] Cascaded shadow maps (3-4 cascades fitted to camera depth slices, texel-snapped, shadow distance with fade) and alpha-masked shadow casters.
   - Why: The single map is fitted to the whole scene, so one far object blurs every near shadow (recorded finding in M8a WP9 and M3 WP5); in any level larger than a room shadows turn to blobs. Masked foliage currently casts solid quads.
   - Depends on: Pass structure (persistent shadow targets, array texture or atlas). A `shadow_cascades` debug view for invariant 8.
   - Risk: Four depth renders per capture on WARP raise CI time; make map size a setting and keep golden scenes small. Keep the nearest comparison sampler and shader-side PCF so adapters compare the same texels. Cascade seams and snapping need structural tests (measure at the shadow tip, as the current tests do).
5. [M | must] Material assets and textures outside glTF: `*.mat.json` (ADR-007/013) with texture slots, UV scale/offset, alpha mode, double-sided, unlit; a way to put a texture on a Primitive; per-entity override of a model's material.
   - Why: Agents and people build most scenes from primitives, and a primitive can only be a flat colour today; a Material placed on a Model is silently ignored. This caps how good anything made inside the engine can look, regardless of the lighting work.
   - Depends on: wish_asset references and `wen mv` rename tracking, edit_apply, editor inspector and a PARITY row, asset_create for placeholder textures, check rules and DIAGNOSTICS entries.
   - Risk: Schema growth of Material changes ABI-visible types (wish_types, SDK prelude, trybuild stderr files); adding fields with defaults keeps old scene files byte-identical, as the Camera change did. Canonical-format and load-save round-trip tests are required for the new file kind.
6. [M | must] Alpha-blended mesh pass: sorted back to front after opaque, depth test without write, in HDR before tone mapping.
   - Why: Glass, water, ghosts, fading objects and most downloaded models with BLEND materials are drawn as solid today (warning only). Developers expect this from any 3D engine.
   - Depends on: Pass structure; material alpha mode (previous item for non-glTF content).
   - Risk: Sort order must be a deterministic function of the scene (distance then entity order as tiebreak). Decide and document how blended surfaces appear in the id buffer and in describe_view visibility fractions; keep the debug views on the same draw list.
7. [M | must] Direct GPU presentation in the player (swapchain, vsync, frame pacing) and interpolation of transforms between the last two ticks, with the readback path kept as the software fallback.
   - Why: Every displayed frame is now a full GPU readback plus a per-pixel CPU copy with no vsync, and motion is shown at whole 60 Hz ticks. On a 1080p or 144 Hz display that reads as stutter and tearing no matter how good the lighting is.
   - Depends on: Pass structure (persistent targets). A presenter in wish_render that accepts a window handle, because wgpu must stay inside wish_render and winit inside wish_window.
   - Risk: Not coverable by CI (the window loop does not run there): needs a local `--exit-after` check and the user's eye. Interpolation is render-only and must never write to the ECS. The editor composites on the CPU (ADR-018) and can stay on readback.
8. [S | must] Render statistics and a render benchmark: draw calls, triangles, culled draws, lights, per-pass time in capture_frame/describe_view output; `wen bench --render` on the hardware adapter.
   - Why: There is no measurement of render cost anywhere in the repository, so performance work would be guesswork, and an agent cannot tell a person why a scene is slow. Cheap, and it extends the AI lead.
   - Depends on: Nothing for counts; timestamp queries are an optional wgpu feature, so fall back to CPU-side timing when absent.
   - Risk: Timings are wall-clock: keep them out of anything hashed, replayed or compared in tests; counts are deterministic and can be asserted.
9. [M | should] Frustum culling (main view and per shadow cascade), opaque draws sorted by pipeline/material/depth, instanced drawing of identical mesh+material, cached sprite and UI atlases.
   - Why: Cost today grows with everything in the scene, not with what is on screen, and each draw rebinds the pipeline. Levels with a few thousand objects will not hold frame rate.
   - Depends on: Render statistics (to prove the gain); bounds already exist per draw in DrawList::push.
   - Risk: Culling must be conservative and CPU-side (deterministic), and the id buffer, describe and debug views must keep using the same draw list or 'what is reported is what is drawn' breaks. Instancing through a storage buffer in the vertex stage stays inside WebGPU core limits.
10. [M | should] MSAA 4x on the HDR and depth targets, resolved before tone mapping.
   - Why: FXAA blurs texture detail and misses sub-pixel geometry; MSAA is what ADR-012 lists in the baseline and what forward renderers are good at.
   - Depends on: Pass structure; the sprite/particle pass depth-tests against the scene depth, so it must run multisampled or against a resolved depth.
   - Risk: Edge coverage between WARP and lavapipe is unmeasured and may exceed the golden tolerance; measure first, and keep goldens on a fixed setting. Id and debug targets must stay single-sample. WebGPU allows only 1 or 4 samples. If measurement fails, amend ADR-012 rather than keep a promise the tests cannot hold.
11. [M | should] Screen-space ambient occlusion (GTAO or SSAO) with a depth pre-pass, applied to indirect light only.
   - Why: Contact darkening in corners and under objects is the cheapest cue that grounds objects; without it IBL-lit scenes look like they float.
   - Depends on: Pass structure, sky/ambient step 1 (so there is an indirect term worth occluding), resolved depth if MSAA is on.
   - Risk: Use a fixed sampling pattern derived from pixel coordinates, no per-frame noise and no temporal accumulation, or single-frame captures stop being repeatable. An `ao` debug view gives the headless check.
12. [M | should] Spot-light shadows (shadow atlas, a bounded number of lights, a `shadows` field on SpotLight); point-light shadows afterwards.
   - Why: Interiors and night scenes are lit by local lights; today those lights shine through walls.
   - Depends on: Cascaded shadows (shares the atlas and filtering code).
   - Risk: Per-frame cost scales with shadowed lights: cap and warn (no silent drop). Point shadows as six atlas faces rather than cube arrays to stay within downlevel limits.
13. [M | should] GPU skinning: joint matrices in a storage buffer, skinned vertex layout, in the opaque, shadow, id and debug pipelines.
   - Why: CPU skinning plus a full mesh upload per animated model per frame limits a scene to a handful of characters.
   - Depends on: Render statistics; the quick win that reuses index and edge buffers buys time before this.
   - Risk: Every pipeline that draws meshes needs the skinned variant or the debug views and id buffer show the bind pose. The existing animation test (pose checked through the id buffer) is the regression guard.
14. [M | should] Device-loss recovery: rebuild the device, pipelines and uploaded models after a loss instead of failing every later call with gpu_lost.
   - Why: Driver updates, sleep/resume and GPU switching happen on players' machines; a shipped game that needs a restart looks broken. Open since M3.
   - Depends on: Pass structure (resources recreated from CPU-side data that the renderer keeps).
   - Risk: Hard to test headless; a forced-loss test hook is needed. Must respect one shared device per process and the gpu_lock rule.
15. [M | should] Particles and import coverage: textured, additive and soft particles; KHR_texture_transform, glTF sampler wrap/filter, vertex colours, second UV set, KHR_materials_unlit, morph targets.
   - Why: Effects are soft discs only, and many downloaded models look wrong (tiling, tint, clamped edges) with only a warning to explain it.
   - Depends on: Post stack A for HDR particles; material assets for sampler settings.
   - Risk: All glTF reading stays inside `guarded` with budgets; each feature needs a generated test model in code (no downloaded assets in the repository).
16. [S | should] Look presets and quality tiers as data: named sets of RenderSettings values shipped as packages, plus Low/Medium/High tiers that scale shadow size, AA and AO.
   - Why: Fits the small-core decision (looks are optional data, not code), gives an agent one word to choose a style, and prepares weaker platforms. Keeps the AI lead: the agent sets a look and verifies it with capture_frame.
   - Depends on: RenderSettings; the M10a package kinds.
   - Risk: Low: data only. Tiers must not change simulation results, only the picture.
17. [L | later] Custom material and post-effect shaders from projects and packages (ADR-013), compressed textures (KTX2/Basis), mesh LOD and occlusion culling.
   - Why: Stylised games (toon, water, dissolve) need their own shaders; large games need texture memory and LOD control.
   - Depends on: Material assets, device-loss recovery, M10c code extensions.
   - Risk: A user shader that naga accepts can still lose a software device (measured on WARP); do not ship before recovery exists. Transcoders add dependencies and binary size, so they belong in optional packages.
18. [XL | later] Global illumination and the heavier effects: baked lightmaps or probe volumes, reflection probes, screen-space reflections, volumetric fog, decals, depth of field, motion blur, TAA and upscaling, auto exposure, GPU-driven drawing, virtual geometry, ray tracing.
   - Why: These are what separate Godot's 6 and Unity's 8 from a solid forward renderer.
   - Depends on: Everything above; mostly already on the M11 list.
   - Risk: TAA, auto exposure and any history-based effect make a capture depend on earlier frames and clash with repeatable single-frame captures unless a fixed warm-up is defined. Compute-heavy paths leave the WebGPU-core baseline (ADR-019).

### Quick wins
- Alpha-masked shadow casters: give shadow_pass.wgsl a fragment stage that samples base-colour alpha and discards below the cutoff (today the shadow pipeline in renderer.rs has `fragment: None` and position-only vertices, so leaves cast solid quads).
- Posed meshes: in Renderer::pose upload only the vertex buffer and reuse the primitive's index and edge buffers; today GpuMesh::new rebuilds the edge list through a BTreeSet and creates three buffers per animated primitive per frame, and again for the id and describe passes.
- Warn when a scene has more than one DirectionalLight: Renderer::render uses `scene.lights.first()` and drops the rest without a diagnostic (add the code to docs::DIAGNOSTICS).
- Fix the stale hint on alpha_blend_not_blended in Renderer::collect ('arrives later (M6)'; M6 is done).
- Hemisphere ambient: blend the sky's zenith and ground colours by the normal's Y in opaque.wgsl instead of one flat Ambient colour; add a sun disc to sky.wgsl from the directional light.
- FXAA as one fullscreen pass after tone mapping, lit view only (pure arithmetic on the 8-bit image, so adapters agree).
- Supersampled captures: a `supersample` parameter on capture_frame that draws at 2x and box-filters on the CPU; adapter-independent anti-aliasing for thumbnails, template pictures and goldens.
- Distance and height fog computed in opaque.wgsl and sky_pass.wgsl from a small Fog resource; vignette, contrast and saturation inside tonemap_pass.wgsl.
- Keep the HDR, depth and shadow textures between frames (keyed by size) instead of creating them in every Renderer::render call.
- Sort opaque draws by pipeline and material and stop calling set_pipeline per draw.
- Apply or reject a Material placed on a Model entity (today Renderer::collect ignores it silently): use it as a tint/override or emit a warning with a hint.
- Read KHR_texture_transform and glTF sampler wrap modes in wish_import (the M3 note says texture transform is supported; the `supported` set in convert() has only KHR_materials_emissive_strength).
- Image statistics in capture_frame/describe_view computed on the read-back image: mean luminance, fraction of clipped and near-black pixels, per-entity mean colour via the id buffer, so an agent can judge exposure and contrast without looking.
- Draw counts in the capture_frame result (draws, triangles, lights, shadow casters): Frame already carries `drawn`.

### Constraints
- GPU rules from M3 WP3: all GPU work under gpu_lock inside Gpu::checked; one Gpu::shared per process; no create_buffer_init (use buffer_with); no shader division that can reach zero and no early return ahead of one (WARP lost the device on exactly that); render tests must also pass with `cargo test --release -p wish_render`.
- Rendering stays outside the determinism boundary: nothing read back from the GPU may enter the world, the hash, a snapshot or a replay; tick interpolation and culling are render-side only.
- Captures must stay repeatable: capture_frame PNG bytes are the same across processes and project scenario tests compare goldens. So no history-dependent effect (TAA, auto exposure, temporal accumulation, per-frame noise) in the capture path unless a fixed warm-up is part of the request.
- Cross-adapter agreement (goldens made on WARP, checked on lavapipe at channel 2 / 0.5%): build lookup data on the CPU (mips, environment prefilter, SH, LUTs), prefer textureLoad with shader-side weights over sampler filtering where the result feeds a golden, keep the nearest comparison sampler for shadows, leave anisotropy off in the tested path; re-run the two-adapter measurement after each effect.
- Every effect gets a headless structural test (on versus off at measured points, as the shadow tests do) plus one small golden, and a debug view or reported value that makes it observable from the control plane (invariant 8). Refresh with UPDATE_GOLDEN=1 and review by eye; look at the Khronos gallery after light or texture changes.
- Schema first and one control plane: every new setting is a documented component or resource in wish_types with units and ranges, set through edit_apply, shown by the schema-generated inspector; a new tool or operation needs a wish_editor::parity::PARITY row and a by-hand test; new diagnostic codes go into docs::DIAGNOSTICS; new interface text into the 11 language files.
- Scene files omit defaults, so changing a schema default changes how every existing scene, example golden and template thumbnail looks. Switch the default look once, deliberately, before 1.0, and refresh all goldens in that one change.
- Baseline stays inside WebGPU core (ADR-019): device is opened with Features::empty() and downlevel_defaults; at most 4 storage buffers per stage (the fragment stage already uses 3), MSAA only 1 or 4 samples, no float32 filtering, no cube arrays assumed, timestamp queries only as an optional extra.
- Dependencies point down: wgpu only in wish_render, winit only in wish_window, the renderer never knows wish_ui or scene files. Direct presentation has to be designed around that split.
- Hostile input: any new importer (HDR environments, compressed textures, user shaders) runs inside `guarded`-style protection with size budgets and returns diagnostics with hints, never a panic.
- Small core (user decision): baseline effects are shader code and data switches inside wish_render with no new heavy dependencies; large optional pieces (environment packs, transcoders, effect packs) belong in packages. Only permissive licences; no large assets in the repository; downloads need the user's approval.
- Software-adapter cost: WARP and lavapipe are slow, and cascades, AO and bloom multiply the work per capture; keep test scenes at small sizes and make shadow resolution a setting so CI time and the disk-space rule hold.
- Decisions before code: ADR-012 lists cascaded shadows, IBL, MSAA and basic post-processing as 1.0 baseline while ROADMAP M11 defers cascades; update docs/DECISIONS.md and docs/ROADMAP.md first, then write a design note per package.

### Notes
Read-only review; nothing was built or run, so no performance figure here is measured. The repository has no render benchmark (crates/wish_cli/src has only bench_script.rs), which is itself a gap.

Recommended order: pass structure and RenderSettings first, because every other item depends on it, then the list as given. The S-size quick wins (masked shadow casters, posed-mesh buffer reuse, hemisphere ambient, sun disc, fog, FXAA, supersampled capture) can land before or alongside it.

Differences from docs/design/M12-closing-the-gaps.md (untracked proposal, packages G1-G3):
- It omits the pass/target foundation, material assets and textures on primitives, alpha-blended meshes, masked shadow casters, the presentation path, culling/instancing/GPU skinning, render statistics and device-loss recovery.
- G3 includes TAA, which conflicts with repeatable single-frame captures; I moved it to 'later'.
- G3's 'every effect off in the deterministic test path unless asked' would leave the tested look different from the shipped look. I recommend effects on by default, tested through adapter-safe implementations.

Not verified:
- How MSAA edge coverage and filtered float targets differ between WARP and lavapipe. This decides whether MSAA and sampler-based bloom can be golden-tested.
- CI time with cascades and post effects on the software adapters.
- The tails of sprites.rs and ui.rs (pipeline creation, per-frame texture upload), most of renderer/debug.rs, and how the editor's game view calls the renderer.
- Whether a checker rule elsewhere already warns about a second DirectionalLight or a Material on a Model. I searched wish_control for the obvious names and found none.

The score of 3 matches the code. The lit path is a correct but minimal forward PBR renderer: one sun, one scene-fitted shadow map, flat ambient, no anti-aliasing, no post effects beyond tone mapping, no transparency and no textures outside glTF. The debug views, id buffer, shader diagnostics and GPU error handling are ahead of the visual features.

## Area: Platform support (key platforms) — score now 1, plausible after 5
Score after assumes: Gaps 1-9 closed: Linux and macOS (arm64) packages of the editor and tools built and smoke-tested in CI and downloadable from Releases; cross-export from any host to Windows, Linux and macOS through optional player packages; swapchain presentation with vsync, fullscreen and HiDPI; gamepad input; Steam Deck checked in the Steam Linux Runtime; compressed base with the compiler as a component. That is roughly where Bevy's desktop support stands, without its web and mobile. If the web preview (gaps 10-12, WebGPU only) also lands, 6 is plausible. Reaching 7-8 needs Android and iOS (gaps 13-14); consoles stay out of reach for an open-source engine without a partner. macOS without notarization and all non-Windows targets without hands-on hardware testing keep the score from going higher than stated.

### Gaps (ordered by the mapper: most score for least work first)
1. [M | must] 1. Linux x86_64: packaged engine (editor, wen, compiler) and exported games. Needed: turn the `output` audio feature on for all desktop targets (wish_player/Cargo.toml 25-26, wish_cli/Cargo.toml 45-46; remove the #[cfg(windows)] around speaker/hear in wish_player/src/lib.rs) and install libasound2-dev in CI; make `wen package` host-aware (skip .wsb/.ps1/.ico and the PE checks, write a .desktop file + PNG icon, emit a .tar.xz so the executable bits survive); build on the oldest supported runner for a low glibc baseline; error display in wish_player/src/main.rs show() for Linux.
   - Why: The cheapest real platform: the code already compiles and passes the whole suite on ubuntu in CI, including the packed compiler. Linux is also the Steam Deck's native target and where most open-source contributors and CI users are.
   - Depends on: Nothing technical. Publishing it depends on gap 2.
   - Risk: Low. cpal pulls ALSA (system library, dynamic). Wayland/X11 differences in winit/softbuffer/arboard are untested by hand (no Linux desktop owned); CI can cover it: xvfb-run `wen view/play/edit --software --exit-after N`, plus the packaged wen in a bare container doing new -> build -> test -> export -> exported `--headless` hash.
2. [S | must] 2. Release pipeline: a tag-triggered workflow that builds wen + wish_player + launcher per platform (Windows with +crt-static), runs `wen package`, runs the clean-environment script against the result, and publishes archives with checksums to GitHub Releases. Today no downloadable build exists for any platform; the only package is a local folder.
   - Why: 'Platform support' is judged by what a person can download. It also replaces the pending manual clean-machine trial with a repeatable job and gives the Hub's engine-versions tab something to install.
   - Depends on: User decisions already listed as waiting in M10b (download address, repository owner for the index). Code signing is optional and the user's.
   - Risk: Low. Runner disk limits (the Linux job already deletes preinstalled SDKs to fit). Unsigned Windows binaries trigger SmartScreen until a certificate exists.
3. [M | must] 3. Cross-export through prebuilt players ('export templates'): `project_export` gets a `target` parameter (windows-x64, linux-x64, macos-arm64, later web). Game code is already Wasm and assets are data, so exporting for another OS is copying a different prebuilt wish_player; nothing is cross-compiled. Players for other targets ship as optional packages through the existing package index. Replace the host cfg!(windows) logic in export.rs (89-93, 223-227, 243) with per-target naming, apply the PE check only to Windows players, write Unix targets as a tar archive (mode bits cannot be set from Windows), and give macOS a .app bundle layout.
   - Why: A Windows user (the primary audience) can ship to Linux, Steam Deck and macOS from one machine, as Godot and Unity allow. It also matches the user's decision: small base, each target an optional component.
   - Depends on: Gaps 1 and 2 (player binaries built in CI), the package index (M10b WP2, exists offline).
   - Risk: Medium-low. Must keep the M9 export rules (side folder then swap, no writes through links, only a checked game.wasm). Determinism: a player of another engine version must be refused (the bit-for-bit promise is per engine build). Parity rule: the editor's Export dialog and a PARITY row for the new parameter.
4. [M | must] 4. Real presentation path and window settings: draw to a GPU surface (swapchain) with vsync instead of readback + softbuffer every frame (viewer.rs 443-471); borderless fullscreen and resolution; scale-factor (HiDPI) handling for games and the editor; window settings in the project file (ProjectConfig has only name/engine/scene/example/packages) exposed through `project_settings`.
   - Why: Players expect fullscreen and smooth frame pacing; the current path costs a GPU-to-CPU copy and a CPU conversion per frame, which is felt most on Steam Deck class hardware. It is also the hard prerequisite for web and mobile, where a blocking readback per frame is impossible. Without HiDPI handling the editor is tiny on 4K and Retina screens.
   - Depends on: Nothing; pairs with the graphics work (post-processing wants the same swapchain).
   - Risk: Medium. Invariant 6: wgpu must stay inside wish_render, so wish_render takes a raw window handle from wish_window. GPU rules: surface work under gpu_lock inside Gpu::checked, one shared device, adapter must now be requested with compatible_surface (gpu.rs 96). The capture/golden path must stay byte-identical; keep softbuffer as the fallback for the software adapter. Surface loss ties into the open 'device loss recovery' item.
5. [M | must] 5. Gamepad input: gamepad button and analog-axis names in wish_input::key_names and in Binding::Axis/Axis2 (dead zone, quantised values), read in wish_window through one wrapper dependency (for example gilrs; license and dependency tree to be checked), editable in the input-map editor, accepted by `sim_step input` and replays.
   - Why: A game engine without controller support cannot target Steam Deck, consoles or couch play; every compared engine has it. It is the single input gap that blocks a 'Deck playable' claim.
   - Depends on: Nothing. Touch (as pointer/click plus multi-touch) can follow the same route later.
   - Risk: Low-medium. Determinism: analog values must be quantised before they enter ActionState and the replay file, so the same recording gives the same hash everywhere. A closed name set means `check`, docs and the 11 language files change together.
6. [S | should] 6. Windows export polish: the exported program is a renamed wish_player with no icon or version resource of the game (wish_player has no build script or resources); no Windows ARM64 build; no installer; unsigned.
   - Why: The first thing a player sees of an exported game is its icon and the SmartScreen warning. Small work, visible on the platform that already works.
   - Depends on: Icon embedding needs a small PE resource writer in the export tool (the engine already parses PE in wish_control/src/pe.rs). Signing needs the user's certificate.
   - Risk: Low. A PE writer must be hostile-input safe and tested headless.
7. [S | should] 7. Steam Deck as a named target: the Linux export run inside the Steam Linux Runtime container in CI, default 1280x800 fullscreen, gamepad actions in the templates, and a documented check list. The proprietary Steamworks SDK stays out of the base and becomes an optional package.
   - Why: Steam is the stated distribution channel and the Deck is the cheapest 'console-like' target; it lifts the platform score visibly for almost no engine work once 1, 4 and 5 exist.
   - Depends on: Gaps 1, 4, 5.
   - Risk: Low for the build; real-device behaviour (performance, suspend/resume, on-screen keyboard) cannot be verified without hardware. Whether today's Windows export runs under Proton was not checked.
8. [L | should] 8. macOS (arm64 first): never compiled in CI. Needed: a macOS CI job; .app bundles with Info.plist and .icns for the engine and for exported games; Command-key mapping (shell.rs 388-405 and app.rs 5285-5293 forward only Ctrl; commands.rs already parses 'Ctrl' as the platform command key); `open` instead of xdg-open in launcher.rs 73; HiDPI from gap 4; ad-hoc signing of wen, the packed rustc and the SDK's proc-macro dylib; notarization when the user has an Apple Developer account.
   - Why: Completes the desktop trio every compared engine except none lacks; many indie developers and most iOS-bound developers work on Macs. It is also the first arm64 target, so it is where cross-architecture determinism gets proven.
   - Depends on: Gaps 1-4. Apple Developer account (paid, the user's) for notarization; without it downloads need a manual approval in System Settings.
   - Risk: Medium-high. No Mac is owned, so verification is CI only. Metal has no software adapter, so golden-image tests cannot run as they do on WARP/lavapipe (hardware Metal on the runner with a tolerance, or hash-only tests); the M3 `no_gpu_adapter` test is documented to fail on macOS. Hardened runtime may refuse rustc loading the proc-macro dylib unless signed consistently. Recorded hashes may reveal an arm64 difference (none is expected from the code read, but it is unproven).
9. [M | should] 9. Small base and optional components: the package is 558 MB and 417 MiB of that is the compiler (rustc_driver ~201 MiB, rust-lld ~117 MiB, wasm32 std ~100 MiB). Ship compressed archives (ADR-025 measured 141 MiB with gzip; xz/zstd smaller), drop the unneeded gcc-ld wrappers, and make the compiler, export players for other targets, and platform SDK glue separate components the Hub installs on demand.
   - Why: The user's explicit decision (small, easy base; the rest optional). Per-platform packages multiply the download unless the heavy parts are shared and optional.
   - Depends on: The download decision that M10b is waiting on (HTTP/TLS library, index address). ADR-023 already allows installing the compiler on first launch.
   - Risk: Medium. Every template has game code, so a base without a compiler must fetch it before the first build and say so with a diagnostic and hint; offline installs need a full archive too. The SDK pack and compiler must stay the same version (script_toolchain_mismatch).
10. [L | should] 10. A lean runtime and a virtual project (enabler for web, mobile, consoles): the player links the whole control plane (tools, docs, packages, scaffolding, the embedded 635 KB license text) and reads a real folder through std::fs (about 260 direct std::fs uses across 53 source files). Extract session build/step (wish_control sessions.rs build_app/step_app and script.rs play_step) into a crate both Workspace and the player use, and read project files through one interface that can be a folder, an archive in memory or an APK asset.
   - Why: A web page or a phone app cannot ship a 30+ crate tool stack or open a folder beside the program. Developers also expect one packed asset file instead of loose files, and small player binaries.
   - Depends on: Nothing, but it should land before any non-desktop target.
   - Risk: Medium-high. It must remain one stepping path (invariant 4 and the determinism promise: the exported game and `sim_step` must be the same code). An archive must be deterministic (sorted, no timestamps) so the replay header's `assets` fingerprint holds.
11. [L | should] 11. A second way to run game code where JIT or wasmtime is unavailable: wish_script is written directly against wasmtime + Cranelift. iOS and consoles forbid JIT (wasmtime's Pulley interpreter is already in Cargo.lock but unused); a browser cannot host wasmtime at all, so the web needs either an interpreter compiled into the player or the browser's own WebAssembly engine with fuel injected at export time.
   - Why: Without it there is no web, iOS or console player however portable the renderer is. It is the one platform blocker that touches the sandbox (invariant 7).
   - Depends on: Gap 10 for the web variant; none for Pulley on native.
   - Risk: High. Fuel is part of observable behaviour: a different executor counts differently, so a runaway system stops at a different instruction and the hash can differ in exactly that case; this must be decided and documented (ADR) rather than discovered. NaN canonicalization is a wasmtime setting; elsewhere the write-back finite-float check is the only guard. Trap-to-file:line diagnostics rely on wasmtime backtraces.
12. [XL | should] 12. Web export (WebGPU first; WebGL2 as a later fallback). Blockers found in the code: blocking GPU calls (pollster::block_on in Gpu::new and in every Gpu::checked, PollType::Wait, readback) cannot run on a browser main thread; presentation is a CPU readback (gap 4); wasmtime (gap 11); std::fs and the full control plane in the player (gap 10); storage buffers for lights (lights.wgsl 18-21) do not exist in WebGL2; audio needs cpal's web backend and a user gesture; winit loops use std::time::Instant.
   - Why: The most visible target for an AI-first engine: a game made in a session becomes a link anyone can open. Unity, Godot, Bevy, GameMaker and Defold all have it.
   - Depends on: Gaps 4, 10, 11. Roughly L once those exist.
   - Risk: High. The GPU rule 'all GPU work inside Gpu::checked' needs a non-blocking form on wasm32 (a design decision, not a port). Invariant 6: wasm-bindgen/web-sys only inside the wrapper crates. Determinism: wasm32 is a new architecture for the engine's own float code (glam/libm and Rapier are built for this, but it is unproven here). This grows the 1.0 scope; a 'web preview, WebGPU only' label would bound it.
13. [XL | later] 13. Android: winit's android-activity and ndk are already in Cargo.lock; wgpu (Vulkan/GLES), Cranelift on arm64 and cpal have Android backends. Missing: touch input, app lifecycle (surface destroyed and recreated on suspend/resume), assets inside the APK (gap 10), a cdylib player per ABI, and APK/AAB assembly and signing.
   - Why: The largest player base; Unity, Godot and Defold are chosen for it. Without mobile the platform score cannot pass about 6.
   - Depends on: Gaps 4, 5 (touch), 10. Android SDK/NDK or a pure-Rust APK assembler and signer.
   - Risk: High. Conflicts with zero-install unless the Android toolchain is an optional component or APK assembly is done by the engine itself. Device loss recovery is still open. Store signing and accounts are the user's.
14. [XL | later] 14. iOS: needs the no-JIT executor (gap 11), touch, lifecycle, an Xcode project or bundle, and Apple signing; realistically exportable only from a Mac.
   - Why: Second half of mobile; expected by commercial developers.
   - Depends on: Gaps 8, 10, 11, 13's shared work; a Mac and an Apple Developer account.
   - Risk: High. Cannot be finished from a Windows machine; interpreter speed for game code on phones is unmeasured.
15. [XL | later] 15. Consoles (Switch, PlayStation, Xbox): platform SDKs are under NDA and cannot live in an open repository; no JIT; Rust std and wgpu have no public console backends. The realistic path is the one Godot and Bevy users take: a third-party port, with the engine keeping its no-JIT executor and virtual-files runtime clean so a port is possible.
   - Why: Matters for commercial releases, not for 1.0 adoption. Unity and Unreal score high here because companies hold the licenses.
   - Depends on: Gaps 10, 11; licensed developer status with each platform holder.
   - Risk: Cannot be verified or even built in public CI. Stating a plan honestly in the docs is the 1.0 deliverable.

### Constraints
- Determinism (invariant 2) is the product's lead: a target counts as supported only when the recorded world hashes and the example replays (collect, hop, islands, bricks, quiz) pass on it in CI. arm64 and wasm32 are new architectures for the engine's own float code; no wall clock or thread timing may enter the simulation when adding window, audio or web layers.
- Game code is untrusted on every target (invariant 7): fuel, memory limit, trap isolation and 'no state in the guest' must hold for any second executor (Pulley, an interpreter, or the browser's engine), and differences in fuel accounting must be an explicit ADR, not an accident.
- Dependencies point down (invariant 6): wgpu only in wish_render; winit, softbuffer, arboard and any gamepad library only in wish_window; cpal only in wish_audio; wasmtime or another executor only in wish_script; wasm-bindgen/web-sys/ndk only inside those wrapper crates.
- GPU rules from M3: all GPU work under gpu_lock inside Gpu::checked, one Gpu::shared per process, no create_buffer_init, render tests must pass in release, golden images compared on the software adapter. A swapchain path must not change what capture_frame produces.
- One control plane and hybrid parity (invariants 4 and 9): every export target is a `project_export` parameter with dry_run, has an editor counterpart and a row in wish_editor::parity::PARITY, new diagnostics go into docs::DIAGNOSTICS with a hint, new interface text into all language files.
- Export rules from the M9 review stay: build in a side folder then swap, never write through links, stay inside the project, `exports/` is never scanned, ship only a game.wasm that equals the loaded module and is newer than its sources.
- A feature is done only when observable from the control plane with a headless test (invariant 8); window loops do not run in CI today, so each new target needs a scripted smoke run.
- Zero install and small base (ADR-023 plus the user's decision): desktop and web export must need no extra SDK; Android/iOS toolchains, Steamworks, anti-cheat and other proprietary or heavy parts are optional components, never in the base. The packed compiler and the SDK pack must be the same rustc version on every host (script_toolchain_mismatch).
- Only permissive licenses (deny.toml: MIT, Apache-2.0, BSD, ISC, Zlib, BSL-1.0, Unicode-3.0); check each new platform dependency before adding it and regenerate THIRD_PARTY_LICENSES.md.
- Things only the user can provide: Apple Developer account (macOS notarization, iOS), Windows code-signing certificate, Google Play and Steam accounts, console developer programs, the download address, and approval for large downloads (Android NDK, emulator images). No Mac, Steam Deck or phone is owned, so those targets are CI-verified only until testers exist.
- Disk and CI limits: the Linux runner already removes preinstalled SDKs (including /usr/local/lib/android) to fit; build outputs must stay out of the OneDrive project folder; run tools/clean-build.ps1 between packages.
- Files are the source of truth (invariant 3): any packed asset format must be a deterministic, lossless container of the project's files so the replay header's asset fingerprint is the same on every target.

## Area: Editor tools (key editor) — score now 4, plausible after 6.5
Score after assumes: Assumes the first thirteen gaps in the order given are closed: G1 navigation, G2 create/duplicate/new scene, G3 HiDPI and menus, G4 overlays and picking, G5 gizmo completion, G6 hierarchy, G7 asset import and management, G9 console/problems/search, G8 play polish with sound, G10 inspector depth, G16 proposal preview, G11 profiler, G15 particle/animation preview. ### Constraints
- Invariants 4 and 9: every project-changing action is one Workspace::call as `human`; a new capability goes into wish_control first, with dry_run (tests/catalog.rs), a PARITY row and a by-hand test (crates/wish_editor/tests/parity.rs), a CLI parity example (wish_cli/tests/parity.rs), diagnostics with hints in docs::DIAGNOSTICS and MCP docs. Editor-only state (camera, selection, layout, snap preferences) needs no call, but must never be the only way to change the project.
- One history: a drag, stroke or paste is one edit_apply sent with the base_revision it started on; previews are drawn on a copy (PictureSource::Preview); Esc leaves no record; actions that touch files are disabled while playing.
- ADR-018 technology: egui 0.37 with the engine's CPU rasterizer and softbuffer. No GPU widgets and no egui paint callbacks (raster draws meshes only); wgpu stays in wish_render. The scene picture is drawn off-screen and blitted, so viewport tools are either CPU pixels on the canvas (as gizmo.rs) or a wish_render pass. Interface budget is 6 ms per frame at 1920x1080 (4.9 ms measured); HiDPI, 4K and long lists threaten it.
- Invariant 8: each feature needs a headless test driven by script input, with widgets found by accessible name, not coordinates; golden images refreshed with UPDATE_GOLDEN=1 and looked at; the window loop does not run in CI.
- Determinism: the editor takes time from the caller; the wall clock is allowed only for the editor's own timings; profiler numbers, debug drawing and audio output must never reach the world, hash, snapshot or replay.
- Schema first: the inspector stays generic; special widgets come from schema attributes, never from per-component editor code; new world types need doc comments, units and ranges.
- GPU rules for any render-side overlay: gpu_lock and Gpu::checked, one Gpu::shared, no create_buffer_init, no division by zero in shaders, tests also in release, overlays off by default so existing goldens and capture bytes hold.
- Interface rules: every string through t()/tf() with 11 translation files (tests/languages.rs), the menu bar must fit 1280 px in every language, colours only through style::pal(), a new icon is style::Icon plus a vector::parts path, a new action gets a COMMANDS row, user guides (en, tr) updated.
- File safety: tool-written paths go through the allow list (normalize_output_path, inside_project, exact_case); overwriting needs an explicit parameter; removal moves to trash; exports/ and .wish/ never enter project scans; outside files only through wish_import::guarded.
- Small base engine (user decision): specialist editors should be self-contained panels that can later ship as packages; that needs the panel registry (G13) and, for code, M10c.
- Dependencies: permissive licenses only (deny.toml); a docking or file-dialog crate needs a license check, and downloads need the user's approval. No async runtime (no tokio).

## Area: 2D games (key two_d) — score now 4, plausible after 7
Score after assumes: Gaps 1 to 7 closed: renderer foundation, sprite sheets and animation, the 2D physics profile on Rapier 3D, tilemaps with collision and painting, fixed viewport with pixel-perfect camera and pointer-in-world, the editor 2D mode, and templates, recipes and evaluation tasks; plus the nine-slice part of gap 8. ### Gaps (ordered by the mapper: most score for least work first)
1. [M | must] 1. Sprite renderer foundation: keep pictures on the GPU (per-picture textures or a cached atlas) instead of rebuilding and uploading a CPU atlas every picture; premultiplied alpha and a texel inset or extruded border so sheet frames do not bleed; mips for smooth sprites; view culling; an explicit order and a Y-sort option inside a layer; sprites and emitters in the id buffer; warnings where things are dropped silently today (atlas full at sprites.rs line 150, particle budget at line 186).
   - Why: Everything else in 2D sits on this. Today a 2048x2048 tileset would be copied texel by texel and uploaded again for each frame, neighbouring sheet frames bleed with `smooth: true`, transparent edges get dark fringes, top-down games cannot sort by Y, an agent cannot learn that one sprite hides another, and a person cannot click a sprite in the editor.
   - Depends on: Nothing. Coordinate with the graphics area on where the sprite pass sits in the render graph.
   - Risk: Golden images change (`sprites.png`, bricks `won.png`) and must be reviewed by eye; the RTX/WARP difference must stay within 1/255 and tests must pass in release; GPU rules (gpu_lock, Gpu::checked, no create_buffer_init). New `Sprite` fields change the world hash of worlds that hold sprites, so saved replays of such games will report divergence.
2. [M | must] 2. Sprite sheets and sprite animation: a typed sheet description (grid slicing, named frames, named clips with rate and loop) in the picture's meta file or a `.sprites.json` asset; a `SpriteAnimation` component shaped like `AnimationPlayer` (clip, time, speed, looping, playing) whose system writes `Sprite.rect` every tick and sends a finished event; Aseprite JSON import; `asset_info` reporting picture size and frames; a tool action to slice and define clips; editor frame picker and clip preview.
   - Why: Every 2D game animates characters. Today game code must do the rect arithmetic by hand each tick (the docs say so), and no tool reports the pixel size of a picture, so an agent has to guess frame rectangles. Godot, Unity, GameMaker and Defold all ship this.
   - Depends on: Gap 1 for bleed-free frames; a typed schema for meta import settings.
   - Risk: Components are fixed-size `Plain` values, so a frame list cannot live in a component: clips go in an asset plus a world extension (ADR-026), and the current frame must be written back to `Sprite.rect` so the hash, snapshots and `expect` see it. The replay `assets` fingerprint must cover sheet files. Sheet JSON is untrusted input and needs guarded parsing and budgets.
3. [M | must] 3. A 2D physics profile on the existing Rapier 3D: a world setting that locks every body to the XY plane (instead of five flags per body), 2D shapes by extrusion (convex polygon), compound and tile colliders (merged rectangles), one-way platforms, a collider sized from the sprite, a checker rule for an unlocked dynamic body under an orthographic camera, point overlap for picking.
   - Why: What the 3D-in-a-plane approach costs today: five lock flags on every body (bricks' ball) with no checker rule if one is forgotten; only Box, Sphere, Capsule and Cylinder, so no slopes except rotated boxes and no tile collision except one collider entity per tile; every collider needs a Z depth; sprite size is in pixels while the collider is in meters, so one rectangle is described twice; no one-way platforms, which platformers expect; more solver work per body than a 2D solver (not measured here; M6 measured 992 3D bodies at 2.0 ms).
   - Depends on: Nothing. Tile collision in gap 4 depends on it.
   - Risk: Recorded physics hashes (`wish_physics/tests/world.rs`, `proof.rs`) must not move for existing scenes, so new fields must default to today's behaviour. One-way platforms need Rapier contact hooks that stay deterministic. Polygon points cannot be a list in a component (fixed array plus count, or an asset). The alternative in the M12 proposal, a Rapier 2D backend with `RigidBody2D`/`Collider2D`, doubles components, the query tool, the character controller and the determinism proofs (L to XL); I recommend it only after 1.0 and only if a benchmark asks for it.
4. [L | must] 4. Tilemaps: a `Tilemap` component (tileset, tile size, layer, collision on or off) with tile data in fixed-size chunk components on child entities, a tileset as a sliced sheet with per-tile collision and tags, one culled mesh per chunk, merged colliders per chunk, control-plane operations to paint, fill and read tiles (including rows of characters with a legend, which suits a language model), a `describe_view` summary, and an editor palette with paint, erase, fill and rectangle tools. Second step: autotile rules and Tiled/LDtk import. Later: isometric and hex.
   - Why: The single biggest missing 2D feature: platformers, top-down games and puzzle games are built on tile grids, and every compared engine with a 2D score of 8 or 9 has a tilemap editor. Today a level is one entity per tile (bricks: 40 entities, an 18 KB scene for 40 rectangles).
   - Depends on: Gaps 1, 2, 3; gap 6 for painting by hand.
   - Risk: The fixed-size `Plain` rule shapes the design: chunks in components keep tiles in the hash, snapshots and `edit_apply`; an asset-plus-extension design would hide tile edits from the hash. Large maps mean large canonical scene files and large diffs. Game code that queries chunk columns copies them across the Wasm boundary every tick (ADR-011 cost, 1 GiB limit). New operations need `dry_run`, a parity example, a `PARITY` row with a by-hand test, and catalogued diagnostics. I did not verify whether the schema has a maximum array length.
5. [M | must] 5. Fixed game viewport, pixel-perfect camera, and the pointer in the world: a viewport policy as world data (design size; fit, integer fit or expand), drawing at a low internal resolution with whole-number nearest upscaling in the player and in captures, camera and sprite snapping to the pixel grid, SDK helpers to turn the pointer into a world position and a world position into screen pixels, and picking the sprite under the pointer.
   - Why: Today the world view takes the window's aspect (`sprites::build(..., aspect)`) while the UI is letterboxed to `UiSettings`, so game code cannot map `Pointer` to a world point: clicking or dragging a sprite (match-3, cards, tower defence, point-and-click) is not possible except through `UiButton`. Pixel art shimmers because nothing snaps to the pixel grid and `smooth: false` is nearest sampling at any scale.
   - Depends on: Gap 1; the present path in `wish_window`/`wish_player` (a CPU blit through softbuffer, where integer upscaling is simple); the platform area's presentation work.
   - Risk: The window's size must never reach the simulation (the UI's rule), so the mapping must be a pure function of `Camera`, the viewport resource and `Pointer`. `capture_frame` at any size must letterbox the same way, or goldens and `describe_view` boxes drift. Recorded pointer values in replays must keep their meaning.

### Constraints
- Determinism (invariant 2): animation time, tile data and the viewport mapping live in the world; simulation paths use only operations that match across platforms (as `wish_text` and `wish_particles` do); no hash-map iteration; draw-time state (parallax offsets, pixel snapping) never feeds back into the simulation.
- Schema first and fixed-size `Plain` components: no lists in components. Frame lists, tile grids and polygon points go into fixed arrays, chunk entities, or asset files with a world extension; under ADR-026 every value the game can see is written back to components each tick, extensions stay out of the hash, and restore-then-continue must be proven bit for bit.
- Files are the source of truth: sheet and tilemap data in canonical JSON, load then save byte-identical, typed meta files, `wen fmt` and `wen check` cover them.
- One control plane and hybrid parity (invariants 4 and 9): slicing a sheet, defining a clip and painting tiles are `wish_control` operations first, each with `dry_run`, a parity example in `wish_cli/tests/parity.rs`, a `wish_editor::parity::PARITY` row with a by-hand test, an actor in the history, and catalogued diagnostics with hints.
- Observable with a headless test (invariant 8): `describe_view` and `entity_get` must show tilemaps, the current animation frame and 2D lights; goldens on the software adapter, refreshed with `UPDATE_GOLDEN=1` and reviewed by eye; render tests pass in release.
- GPU rules: all GPU work under `gpu_lock` and `Gpu::checked`, no `create_buffer_init`, no division by zero in WGSL, WebGPU-core features only (ADR-012, ADR-019) so a web target stays possible, RTX and WARP within 1/255.
- Dependencies point down: new 2D crates talk through the ECS and `wish_types` (which must compile on wasm32); outside libraries only inside wrapper crates and inside `guarded` calls; licences MIT, Apache-2.0 or BSD only (cargo-deny), which argues for reading Aseprite and Tiled JSON ourselves.
- Game code is untrusted: any new host import (for example tile access) needs fixed fuel, budgets and hostile-module tests; existing limits apply (65,536 commands per call, 1 GiB of component data).
- Hash stability: new fields on `Sprite`, `UiImage`, `UiNode` or `PhysicsSettings` must default to today's behaviour so scene files stay byte-identical (defaults are not written); recorded physics hashes must not move; saved replays of games that use the changed types will report divergence.
- Small base (user decision): the 2D kit as separate crates and as data packages (templates, tilesets, recipes). Code extensions (M10c) are after 1.0, so 'optional' in 1.0 means crate boundaries and packages, not runtime plugins.
- Editor rules: text through `t()` in all 11 language files, a command id per action, colours through `pal()`, icons as vector parts, tests find widgets by name, file-touching actions disabled while playing.
- This grows the 1.0 scope (the M12 proposal already says so): which 2D gaps enter 1.0 is the user's decision.

### Constraints
- One control plane: components, downloads and verification are `wish_control` tools first. A new tool needs a parity example (wish_cli/tests/parity.rs), a `wish_editor::parity::PARITY` row with a by-hand test, `dry_run` on every mutating action (tests/catalog.rs), its codes in `docs::DIAGNOSTICS`, texts in all 11 language files and the guides' button names.
- Amend the documents before the code: VISION and ADR-023 ('a single package'), ADR-022 ('local network only') and the 'nothing is sent' privacy wording. Outbound requests only fetch, only on a person's action, with no identifiers.
- Person-only where the computer changes for every project: user-scope packages, engines, and so components. Agents get a diagnostic with a hint. Nothing is deleted (trash); large files go to the machine folder (`crash::machine_folder()`), not the roaming one.
- Determinism: the compiler component must be the pinned rustc that built the SDK (the `script_toolchain_mismatch` check stays); project packages are pinned by `wish.lock`; a verdict holds only for the same engine version and build.
- Game code is untrusted and there is no native plugin: kits and extensions are Wasm under fuel and memory limits; every compiled source passes `source_check`; a module is checked before it becomes `game.wasm`; hostile-input tests are part of each closing review.
- Indexes, manifests and archives are hostile input: paths stay inside (`inside_path` plus real-folder checks), sizes are capped, links refused, hashes checked in the side folder before the move, `wish.` ids only from the engine's own folder.
- No `panic = "abort"` for `wen` or the player: `Workspace::call` and `wish_import::guarded` catch panics.
- Licences: only what `deny.toml` allows; regenerate THIRD_PARTY_LICENSES.md when dependencies change; the compiler component must carry rustc's and LLVM's licence texts; asset packs' attributions must reach exported games.
- Old projects stay byte-identical: `packages` in `wish.json` only when non-empty, no `wish.lock` without packages, canonical JSON; `exports/` is never scanned; an export is built in a side folder.
- Hybrid parity: every package, component and verification action is available by hand in the Hub or editor, and enters the same history with its actor.
- Large downloads and installs on the development machine, CI spending and evaluation runs need the user's approval.

### Notes
(a) Modular install. Two concepts kept apart: packages (cross-platform data and sandboxed code, by anyone) and components (platform-specific binaries, by the engine only). Base, about 68 MiB by the given figures: launcher, `wen`, host player, engine packages, templates with built modules, embedded docs and licences. On demand: `toolchain` (shared per rustc version), `sdk` (per engine version), `export.<target>` players, heavy sample content. Native subsystems (physics, audio, UI) stay compiled in: together with the editor they are the 68 MiB, Rust has no stable ABI for loadable modules, the project forbids native plugins, and a world hash per subsystem combination would multiply the test matrix. 'Optional' therefore means the compiler, export targets, content, kits and tools. Steam and offline users get the full bundle in the same layout.

(b) Code plugins. For 1.0: `assets`/`prefabs` kinds and experimental kits (source compiled with the game). `wish_ext` tool plugins after 1.0; its smallest useful form is tool-only, prebuilt Wasm, no panels. This moves M10d ahead of M10c and grows the 1.0 scope.

(c) Verification. It proves that a given input sequence produces a given result under one exact build, by re-simulating on the server with the server's copy of the game; a score sent by the client is never trusted. Honest limits: it cannot tell a human from a bot, a macro or tool-assisted input; it hides no information (wallhacks); it does nothing for real-time multiplayer (the engine has no networking); seeds must come from the server or runs can be precomputed; it needs the same engine version and build. Determinism is recorded for Windows and Linux on x86-64; ARM and a browser's Wasm engine (NaN payloads, no fuel) are unproven, so verify on x86-64 native only.

What grows an ecosystem, in order: something to install on day one, a documented format with a scaffold command, a free index with one-step publish, assets and behaviour as shareable units, visible releases, and a place where authors meet.

Not checked: individual file sizes in C:\wish-build\package (totals are from the task text and docs); whether `.rmeta` files are needed by the build; whether module bytes depend on the project path; size gain from a release profile; licence fit of any TLS crate; replay verification throughput. No build, test or engine command was run. Early on I used three read-only shell listings (wc, ls, grep) besides Read/Grep/Glob; nothing was written.

   - Why: Examples are both the first impression and the copy-paste source for a first game. A developer judges 'can this engine make my game' from them, and an agent learns the engine's idioms from them.
   - Depends on: Graphics packages G1-G3 for the look; CC0 art (user approval for downloads, licence recorded in the repository); the package index, so that heavy showcase assets ship as optional packages and the base stays small.

   - Why: Maturity is the part of this score that code cannot produce. Bugs found by strangers on strange machines, and the time to fix them, are what make a 1.0 believable.
   - Depends on: The release pipeline; the user (testers, name decision before the docs and site are published, code signing, announcing a beta).

   - Depends on: The docs site; part of the list can be generated from the diagnostics catalogue.
   - Risk: Must be updated with every package that removes a limit; put it on the release checklist.
13. [M | should] No editor support for hand-written game code. A project has only scripts/src/lib.rs (no Cargo.toml by design, ADR-025); scaffold.rs writes no rust-project.json; the SDK pack holds .rlib/.rmeta and macro DLLs but no sources; the packed compiler has no standard-library sources.    - Depends on: Shipping SDK sources and a subset of rust-src in the package; scaffold writing rust-project.json and an editor settings file; `wen build` output in a form editors can parse.

   - Why: An interactive first lesson inside the product converts far more first runs into first games than a document does, and it would be a place where the AI mode and the manual mode are taught side by side.
   - Depends on: Tutorials written first; a mission as a data file (steps plus a check expression the control plane evaluates, such as `count(Coin) == 3`), shipped as a package content kind.

15. [M | should] Quality signals are not public or tracked per release. Eval results are JSON files in evals/results; `wen bench` numbers are recorded only in design notes; there is no page that shows pass rate, cost, test count and benchmark per version, and no evaluation that follows the tutorials as a newcomer would.
   - Why: The engine's strongest claims (determinism 10, AI 9) are measurable; showing the measurements per release is a maturity signal competitors do not have.
   - Depends on: The docs site; the 20 new tasks of G8; user approval for every paid eval run.
   - Risk: Eval runs spend the user's quota; publishing a comparison written by the home side invites criticism unless the limits section stays attached.
16. [L | later] Documentation languages. The interface has 12 languages, the guide two, and diagnostics, SDK reference and recipes are English only (already on the M11 list).

### Gaps (ordered by the mapper: most score for least work first)
1. [S | must] Ritual and tool-loading turns. In the M7 run (41 tasks, 473 calls) 127 calls (27%) carried no task work: ToolSearch 54 (the client loading deferred tools; first call in 40 of 41 tasks, a second one mid-task in several), check 38 (each answered 76 characters, passed), project_info 35. The project CLAUDE.md that scaffold.rs writes and the loop in docs::guide() order all three. Fix: rewrite both as the reference card INSTRUCTIONS already is; let edit_apply, script_build and test_run answers carry the error and warning count of what they touched so a closing check is not needed; fold the default scene's outline into project_info when the scene is small; advertise a small core tool set that the client can load up front.
   - Why: Each turn re-reads about 31 thousand tokens of client context, so turn count is the cost. Removing these three alone cuts about a quarter of calls on every task, for every user, without touching task success.
   - Depends on: Nothing for the doc and answer changes. Up-front loading depends on what the client offers (see notes: not verified here).
   - Risk: A problem count on every edit means a project scan; limit it to the touched documents. Dropping the check ritual must not hide project-wide errors: keep check in the verification gate (below). Per MCP_API.md, revert if evaluation numbers get worse.
2. [M | must] The game-code loop wastes the most. Game-code tasks took 12-28 calls against 5-10 for scene tasks. Seen in transcripts: (1) whole doc pages read into context: 13 resource reads averaging 11.4 kB, up to 17.6 kB (30% of all answer bytes, 42% without pictures), because docs_search returns a 200-character snippet and needs every word to match (docs.rs search_with; 'impulse rigidbody velocity apply' found nothing); (2) the test file format had to be looked up (first file written without format: 1; test_run's description has no JSON example); (3) a failed until/expect gives values only at the end, so 16_jump_code opened a session and spent 8 calls to learn the jump peak; (4) test_run and sim_start never notice game code edited after the last build. Fix: docs_search ranks by any word and returns the whole section; one resource per recipe; test_run takes an inline test (and save_as), defaults format, shows a JSON example, and builds stale code itself through the script_build path (or refuses with a hint); failures carry a short trace (min, max and tick of each path the condition reads over the run step).
   - Why: Writing game code is where a small game is actually made and where cost and failures concentrate; it is also where the lead over Unity is not yet measured.
   - Depends on: sources_newer_than in tools/export.rs; documents::normalize_output_path for save_as; the build path of tools/script.rs.
   - Risk: Auto-build must keep the M5 rule (side file, checked, then game.wasm). Inline tests write files: path allow list, no overwrite without a parameter, dry_run. ### Gaps (ordered by the mapper: most score for least work first)
1. [S | must] Simulation, test and replay tools can run stale game code. sim_start, test_run and replay run use Workspace::game_module() (the last build) with no check; only project_export compares source times (sources_newer_than in tools/export.rs). A module built by another process (CLI, a second MCP server) is not noticed because load_built_module runs only at open. Proposal: a content fingerprint of scripts/**/*.rs (StableHasher) stored beside game.wasm, one Workspace::ensure_code_fresh() used by sim_start, test_run, replay run and export, auto-build by default with compile errors returned instead of running old code, and project_info.scripts.stale.
   - Why: An agent edits lib.rs, forgets script_build, runs test_run and gets a pass on the old code: a silent wrong answer in the engine's strongest loop. It also turns edit -> build -> test (two calls) into one call.
   - Depends on: Nothing.
   - Risk: sim_start and test_run are declared non-mutating; building inside them writes .wish/build (a cache, not a project file) and must be allowed in read-only and suggest-only permission modes or refused with a clear diagnostic. No determinism risk if the fingerprint is content based, not time based.
2. [S | must] The shipped player does tool work every frame. Workspace::play_step calls step_session, which runs inspect() (world.get_json on every entity's Transform plus a name allocation per entity), computes the full app hash and counts every event type twice, then throws the hash away. Proposal: a lean play path (no hash, inspector only in develop mode or every N ticks).
   - Why: By the M1 measurement the world hash alone is 5.5 ms at 100,000 entities, a third of the frame, spent on nothing in every exported game; the JSON inspector grows with entity count too.
   - Depends on: Nothing.
   - Risk: Low; the hash is read-only. Keep the inspector findings available in the editor and tools.
3. [M | must] No profiler from the control plane. CallReport::fuel_used is computed per game system call and dropped in ScriptRuntime::run; App::step records no per-system time; the editor panel shows only interface/scene/game ms; there is no benchmark that plays a real project. Proposal: per-system wall time kept in the step report (never in the world), fuel per game system (deterministic, so tests can assert budgets), a `profile` option on sim_step, per-system bars in the editor, and `wen bench --project` that plays a replay headless.
   - Why: A developer whose game drops frames cannot see which system costs what; Unity, Unreal and Godot all ship a profiler. Every other performance item below needs this to be measured.
   - Depends on: Nothing.
   - Risk: Wall time must stay outside the world, hash and snapshots and must never be readable by systems (invariant 2). Prefer a parameter on sim_step over a 35th tool (ADR-010).
4. [M | must] Builds block and the editor does not watch code. build_game_within waits on the calling thread, so the first 7.5-8.8 s build freezes the editor frame (welcome.rs builds right after project creation) and an MCP server; editor Play does not rebuild on save although `wen play` does. Proposal: a background build owned by the workspace (result applied at the next tick or call boundary), a code watch as a control-plane capability used by the editor, `wen play` and the HTTP endpoint, the Problems tab filled automatically, a build status chip.
   - Why: Save-and-see without pressing anything is what earns Godot and Defold their 9. A frozen window during the first build is the first impression of a new user.
   - Depends on: The stale-code fingerprint (first gap).
   - Risk: A build thread must hand its result over only at a tick boundary. The watch must live in wish_control, not only in the editor (invariant 4). File actions are disabled while playing; hot reload during play is already allowed.
5. [S | should] First build and no-op builds cost more than needed. A fresh install pays 7.5-8.8 s (cold load of the 201 MiB compiler library); every build spawns `rustc -vV` first (rustc_version) and then the compiler even when no source changed. Proposal: templates ship a prebuilt module with its source fingerprint, the compiler is warmed in the background when a project opens, unchanged sources skip the compiler, the version check is cached per toolchain.
   - Why: The vision promises download to first running scene in under five minutes and a loop measured in seconds; the first Play of a template should be instant.
   - Depends on: The stale-code fingerprint.
   - Risk: A prebuilt module must be rejected when the SDK or ABI differs (the checks exist: script_abi_mismatch, script_type_mismatch).
6. [M | should] Wasm boundary cost is about 15 times the raw ABI cost: 240 ns per entity against 13-15 ns measured for the hand-written ABI in M5 WP1. Causes in code: per-value Plain::decode into a Vec and encode of every row (wish_sdk/src/query.rs), row-by-row column copies, a full frame clone for diffing, a full read-back of read-only columns (wish_script/src/game.rs). The slice-view work carried from M5 to M6 was not done. Proposal, in order: slice views for padding-free types; read-only columns neither copied back nor compared; Changed/Added filters in the SDK (the host already has query_since and last_run); frame buffers reused across ticks; columns written straight into guest memory.
   - Why: From the recorded numbers, five read+write systems over 10,000 entities would take roughly 70% of a 60 Hz frame (arithmetic, not a measurement). This is the ceiling on how big a scripted game can be.
   - Depends on: The profiler, to prove each step; a fuel-budget test to keep it.
   - Risk: More unsafe in wish_sdk (slice casts over guest memory): Miri and SAFETY comments required. Write-back validation (finite floats, valid bool/enum) and the changed-rows-only rule must survive. Any frame layout change is an ABI change: version bump, `wen sdk`, rebuild of all examples.
7. [M | must] No save games. World snapshots exist only in memory (sessions 'never write to disk'); game code has no file access by design, and the SDK has no save/load. Proposal: engine-owned save slots written from a snapshot in a versioned canonical format, loaded with the same field-by-field migration hot reload uses (World::retype_*), requested by game code through a command or event and observable from the control plane.
   - Why: A game that cannot keep progress, settings or a high score between runs cannot ship. Every engine in the comparison has it; here it is a short step from what snapshots already do.
   - Depends on: A decision on physics state: serialize Rapier's state, or rebuild it from components with wish_physics::prepare.
   - Risk: Rebuilding physics loses warm-start state, so a loaded game is not bit-identical to an uninterrupted one; that must be stated and replays across a load need a rule. Save files are untrusted input: validate like scene files. Save location must stay out of project scans (the exports/ rule). Likely overlaps the game-systems area.
8. [M | should] Replays are a development tool, not a product feature. The exported player cannot record or play a replay; recording starts only at tick 0; every tick stores a full-world hash; the limit is 54,000 ticks (15 minutes); there is no seeking. Proposal: player options to record and play, an always-on ring of recent input kept by the player and written beside the crash report on a failed system, hashes every N ticks (replay format 2), periodic keyframes for seeking and longer runs, the game's version stamped in the header.
   - Why: 'Send me the replay' bug reports, ghosts, kill-cams and shareable runs are things no engine in the comparison gives for free; this is what makes the 10 visible to players and not only to tests.
   - Depends on: Lean play path; a serialized snapshot format (shared with save games) for keyframes.
   - Risk: A replay is valid only for the same code, scene and assets (the header already carries all three); recorded hashes are 64-bit short hashes of the app hash, so a format change needs a version bump. Replay files from players are untrusted input.
9. [M | should] Time-travel debugging is coarse. The editor timeline stops taking snapshots after 30 (60 s of play), rewinds only to 2 s marks, truncates the future, and records input only when Record is on. There is no step back by one tick, no 'go to tick T', no world diff between two ticks, no 'which system wrote this value'. Proposal: always log input during editor play, keep a thinning ring of snapshots, go-to-tick as restore plus deterministic re-run, a session diff in the control plane, and 'last written by system X at tick T' derived from the per-row change ticks (App::step advances the change tick after every system).
   - Why: Scrubbing a bug backwards and asking who changed a value is a debugging experience Unity, Unreal and Godot do not have; it is the editor-side proof of the determinism lead and directly useful to agents ('what changed between tick 410 and 412').
   - Depends on: Nothing hard; cheaper with the profiler and lean step path.
   - Risk: New control-plane abilities need PARITY rows, CLI parity and headless tests; diff answers must respect the token budget (ADR-010). Snapshot memory grows with world size (full clones).
10. [L | must] Presentation path and frame pacing in the player: every frame is rendered off screen, read back to the CPU, converted pixel by pixel and shown through softbuffer (viewer.rs redraw); the loop polls without vsync; rendering shows the last tick with no interpolation although ADR-005 says render interpolates and App::update already computes alpha.
   - Why: Readback cost grows with resolution and is paid in every exported game; a 60 Hz simulation on a 120/144 Hz screen judders. This is the largest fixed cost in the real frame budget.
   - Depends on: The graphics and platform plans (a wgpu surface inside wish_render); belongs mostly to that area.
   - Risk: wgpu must stay inside wish_render (invariant 6) and the GPU rules (gpu_lock, Gpu::checked) apply. Interpolation is render-only and must not touch the world. Headless capture must stay the tested path.
11. [M | should] Input and replay format are single-player: one ActionState resource, one Values map per tick, no gamepad. Proposal: make actions and the replay format player-indexed now (player 0 by default), which also gives local multiplayer.
   - Why: It is the one piece of rollback-netcode groundwork that is cheap before 1.0 and a breaking change after it (SDK Actions API, replay files, sim_step input). Couch co-op is expected of any engine at 1.0.
   - Depends on: Replay format 2 (do both format changes together).
   - Risk: ActionState is in the world hash and snapshots: recorded hashes change, so version tags and all example tests and replays must be refreshed. Gamepad support needs a dependency licence check.
12. [S | should] Determinism and performance are not guarded across machines in CI. No replay file is committed (examples record and replay within one run), so whole-game cross-OS determinism (D2, a goal in ADR-005) is unproven beyond the physics pyramid; neither bench runs in CI. Proposal: one committed replay per example played on Windows and Linux, fuel budgets asserted for the bench systems, bench output kept as an artifact.
   - Why: Shared replays, verified leaderboards and netcode all assume the same result on another machine; it is cheap to learn now where that fails.
   - Depends on: Nothing.
   - Risk: It may fail and expose a leak (std sin/cos are used in wish_particles, glam uses libm); that is the point. Fuel budgets are stable across machines, wall times are not, so only fuel should gate.
13. [M | should] The compiler is the bulk of the install: 417 MiB of the 513 MB package. Proposal: make the Rust toolchain an optional engine package; the base engine opens, edits, plays prebuilt projects and exports without it, and offers to add it the first time code must be built.
   - Why: The user asked for a small base with everything else optional; this single move changes the 'lightness and install' score more than anything else in this area.
   - Depends on: Package and engine downloads (M10b, waiting for the user's decision); prebuilt template modules.
   - Risk: Works against ADR-023 'zero install' unless the add is one click and offline bundles exist. Version pinning between SDK pack and compiler (script_toolchain_mismatch) must hold for a separately delivered toolchain.
14. [M | should] Game modules are JIT-compiled at every start (wasmtime::Module::new with Cranelift) and the player ships the compiler backend. Proposal: precompile at export and load the precompiled module in the player, behind a small host interface so a platform without JIT (or the browser, which brings its own Wasm engine) can supply another backend.
   - Why: Platform support is the user's top priority: iOS and consoles forbid JIT and the web cannot run wasmtime. It also shortens game start and shrinks the player.
   - Depends on: The platform plan; web export (XL) builds on it.
   - Risk: Loading precompiled code is unsafe (allowed only in wish_script) and must be tied to the exact engine build. Browser engines have no fuel: limits there need instrumentation, and equal behaviour across engines is a D2 question for replays.
15. [L | later] Everything runs on one thread: systems, the single per-tick guest instance (one Mutex slot), physics, CPU skinning, UI layout, transform propagation (walks every entity each tick, no dirty tracking) and render extraction. Proposal in two steps: first a simulation thread and a render thread (no effect on determinism); later a scheduler that runs consecutive systems with disjoint declared access in parallel and merges write-back and commands in system order (the manifest already declares reads and writes).
   - Why: The development machine has 12 threads and the engine uses one; Bevy and Unity DOTS sell exactly this. The split alone roughly doubles frame headroom.
   - Depends on: Profiler; direct presentation path.
   - Risk: Parallel systems are the highest determinism risk in this list: text interning writes to the world during a call through a raw pointer (host.rs WorldRef) and physics queries read it, so both need ordering rules. ADR-005 allows only disjoint access with fixed-order merges. 'Parallel system execution' is on the M11 list.
16. [XL | later] No scripting option without a compiler. Game code is Rust only and cannot use third-party crates (ADR-025 cost). Proposal: a scripting language whose interpreter is itself a prebuilt Wasm guest under the same ABI, fuel and sandbox, with scripts as project data; a design spike now so that manifest and system declaration stay language-neutral.
   - Why: Instant edit-run with no toolchain is why Godot scores 9 and it is the only route to code iteration on platforms where rustc cannot run (web editor, tablets). It also lowers the bar for people who write by hand.
   - Depends on: A stable ABI; boundary-cost work (an interpreter multiplies per-entity cost).
   - Risk: A second language doubles SDK docs, recipes and evaluation tasks; interpreted code is far slower per entity; candidates must be MIT/Apache/BSD and build for wasm32 without WASI (not evaluated here). On the M11 list; also the fallback named in ADR-023.
17. [L | later] Rollback netcode and replay verification as optional packages: a rollback driver (snapshot ring per tick, re-simulate with corrected input, sound events not repeated), a headless verifier that re-runs a submitted replay and confirms its result (the basis for cheat-proof leaderboards, the user's anti-cheat example).
   - Why: This is the product form of the determinism lead that other engines cannot copy cheaply.
   - Depends on: Player-indexed input, replays in the player, cross-OS determinism proof, a Linux headless player, lean step path and boundary cost (re-simulating N ticks inside one frame).
   - Risk: Full-clone snapshots (about 3 ms at 100,000 entities plus the Rapier state) bound world size for rollback. Networking itself is outside 1.0 (VISION).
18. [S | later] Physics scale is modest and fixed by the determinism build: about 1,000 active bodies cost 12% of the frame, scalar and single-threaded. Proposal for 1.0: documented budgets, a physics row in the profiler, solver settings exposed; 2D physics for 2D games is already proposed (M12 G6).
   - Why: Developers need to know the envelope before they design a game around it.
   - Depends on: Profiler.
   - Risk: A faster SIMD or threaded physics mode would change results across platforms and break shared replays; it needs its own ADR and is not proposed for 1.0.
19. [M | must] Game code cannot switch scenes at run time: a session is bound to one scene, a replay header names one scene, and neither the SDK nor wish_types has a load-scene command (searched; none found).
   - Why: A game with a menu and more than one level needs it; it also defines what a replay and a save cover.
   - Depends on: Replay format 2 and save games (a scene switch must be a recorded, deterministic step).
   - Risk: Probably also mapped by the game-systems area; listed here because the session, replay and snapshot model must change with it.

### Constraints
- Determinism (invariant 2, ADR-005): no wall clock, unseeded randomness or thread-timing dependence in simulation code. Profiling times must live outside the world, hash and snapshots; fuel is the deterministic measure. Parallel execution only for systems with disjoint access, with write-back and commands merged in fixed order.
- Recorded hashes and formats: a change to what is hashed or stored needs a version bump (wish-world-hash-v1, wish-app-hash-v1, replay `format`) and a deliberate refresh of the recorded values in wish_ecs/tests/snapshot.rs and wish_core tests.
- Game code is untrusted (invariant 7): source_check, cleared environment, 120 s build limit, side-file build then install, write-back validation (finite floats, valid bool/enum, changed rows only, all checked before any write) and the limits (65,536 commands per call, 4,194,304 entities, 1 GiB component data, hierarchy depth 256, 16 MiB of types) must survive every boundary optimization.
- Guests are stateless (ADR-011, ADR-025): one fresh instance per tick; no optimization may carry guest memory across ticks unless memory is reset (ADR-025 trigger: instance cost above 5% of the budget).
- One control plane, observable and tested headless (invariants 4, 8, 9): auto-build, code watch, profiler, replays in the player, save/load and time travel go into wish_control first; each new tool or action needs a wish_editor::parity::PARITY row with a by-hand test, a CLI parity example (wish_cli/tests/parity.rs), dry_run on mutating actions, and new diagnostic codes in docs::DIAGNOSTICS with a hint.
- Keep the tool surface small (ADR-010, 34 tools today): prefer parameters on sim_step, replay, script_build and entity_get over new tools; answers stay within the token budget.
- Crate boundaries (invariant 6): wasmtime only in wish_script, wgpu only in wish_render (the presentation fix included), Rapier only in wish_physics; unsafe only in wish_ecs, wish_script and wish_sdk with SAFETY comments and Miri runs.
- World extensions (ADR-026): anything kept outside columns must restore and continue bit-identically to never having gone back, proven by a test; take extensions with World::take_extension instead of cloning.
- ABI v1 and the SDK pack are tied to the pinned rustc (script_toolchain_mismatch); a frame layout change needs an ABI version bump, `wen sdk`, and a rebuild of every example and recipe (tests/recipes.rs compiles them).
- Files are the source of truth: sessions never write project files; builds write only under .wish/build; player saves and replays need a place that project scans skip, written through the allow-listed output paths.
- Dependencies: MIT/Apache/BSD only (deny.toml), `python tools/third_party.py` after any change; no downloads or installs without the user's approval; evaluation runs spend the user's quota and need approval.
- User decisions: small base engine with optional packages; graphics and platforms come first, so items here that unblock platforms (precompiled modules, direct presentation, optional toolchain) rank above pure speed work.
- Editor rules: new interface text through t()/tf() with all language files, new actions as rows in commands::COMMANDS, file-touching actions disabled while playing.

The working tree has uncommitted efficiency work (sim_step `watch`, edit_apply `now`, docs/design/ai-efficiency.md, crates/wish_control/tests/efficiency.rs); the stale-code guard and one-call build+test fit the same goal of fewer agent turns.

## Web research: table_stakes

### Table stakes
- **Rendering**: PBR materials matching glTF 2.0 (normal, occlusion, emissive, alpha modes, clearcoat is becoming common); Directional, point and spot lights with shadows on all of them (cascaded sun shadows); area lights arrived in Godot 4.7; Some global illumination answer: baked lightmaps or probes at minimum; real-time GI is the high-end differentiator (Lumen, Surface Cache, Solari); Reflections: probes plus screen-space reflections; Post stack: modern tonemapper (ACES/AgX), bloom, ambient occlusion, anti-aliasing (MSAA/TAA/FXAA), an upscaler (FSR/DLSS), fog; LOD, occlusion culling, instancing, GPU particles, decals; Skeletal animation with blending and IK; morph targets; Shader authoring in text and a visual graph, with live preview; A mobile/low-end renderer tier alongside the desktop one; HDR output and D3D12/Vulkan/Metal backends are now expected on desktop
- **Platforms and export**: Windows, macOS, Linux from one click, with export templates fetched per platform; Steam Deck readiness: native Linux or clean Proton run, gamepad with correct glyphs, legible at 1280x800; Android and iOS; Web export that works on portals and iOS Safari (single-threaded WebGL 2 build; WebGPU is arriving, not yet required); A console path: first-party in Unity, Unreal, GameMaker Enterprise and Defold; third-party under NDA for open-source Godot; Controller database support (SDL mappings), code signing and notarization, patch or delta packs
- **2D tooling**: Sprites, atlases, sprite-sheet animation editor; Tilemap editor with autotiling/terrains and layers; 2D physics (Box2D class) with one-way platforms; 2D lights and shadows, particles, parallax, y-sort, pixel-snap; 2D skeletal or cut-out animation; Importers for Aseprite, Tiled and LDtk (community plugins are accepted); Nine-patch and resolution-independent UI
- **Editor tooling**: Scene tree, inspector, viewport gizmos with snapping, undo/redo everywhere; Play in editor with a live remote inspector; Script debugger with breakpoints and stepping; CPU/GPU/memory profilers; Animation timeline and curve editor; visual shader editor; tilemap and UI layout editors; Asset browser with previews and quick-open; command palette; dockable, themeable layout; Input map, project settings, localization tools; Text-friendly scene files that survive version control; Accessibility of the editor itself (screen-reader support has started to appear)
- **Scripting and iteration**: A high-level language with hot reload measured in seconds or less; Fast enter-play (Unity is making it default; near-instant is the stated goal); Typed option and IDE/language-server integration; Native extension interface for performance code; A test framework and headless runs for CI; Migration guides and upgrade tools between versions; stability promises after 1.0
- **Asset pipeline**: glTF 2.0 as a first-class format, FBX accepted, documented Blender workflow; Automatic reimport on change with stable asset IDs that survive moves; Texture compression (BCn, ASTC, Basis) and mip generation; LOD generation; Audio (wav, ogg, mp3), fonts, import presets; Skins, animation clips, morph targets from glTF; Localization tables (CSV/PO)
- **Documentation and learning**: Step-by-step 'your first 2D game' and 'your first 3D game'; Complete API reference searchable inside the editor; Docs versioned per engine release, with migration guides; Sample projects and starter templates that actually run on the current version; Community spaces and translated docs
- **Plugins and stores**: In-editor browser for add-ons and assets with ratings, reviews and per-engine-version downloads; Editor plugin API; Clear licences per asset; Paid assets (Unity Asset Store, Fab; planned for Godot); Labelling and filtering of AI-generated content is now expected by users
- **AI-assisted development (2026 baseline)**: An official or sanctioned agent surface: MCP server and/or a CLI with structured output (Unity, Unreal, GameMaker, Defold all have one; Godot and Bevy rely on the community); Agent can read the scene tree, edit scenes and scripts, read console errors, run the game and get a screenshot; Client approval or token before an outside process can drive the editor; Custom, project-specific tools; Agent guidance files (AGENTS.md, skills) and a docs lookup tool; Opt-in: every vendor that shipped AI in 2026 stresses it can be ignored

### Opportunities
- Make 'the agent can prove it works' the headline. Benchmarks put the failure in runtime and visual verification (code scores 70-90 versus screenshot scores 0-20; best agent 41% on full Godot games), and incumbents' agent surfaces mostly edit files and scenes. An engine where every run is deterministic, headless capture and view description are built in, and scenario tests and replays are first-class closes exactly that gap.
- Headless that needs no display, no licence and no sidecar. Godot's headless mode drops button clicks and crashes on movie capture, Unity needs a Build Server licence per headless instance, community MCP servers need Node or Python. A single binary that simulates, renders with a software adapter and returns the same bytes in CI is a concrete, demonstrable advantage.
- A first-party agent surface that is free, local and hardened from day one. Unity's MCP was paywalled at launch and drew a backlash; Epic's is experimental with one undo entry per call and unfinished hardening; Godot has none. Dry-run on every changing action, permission modes, checkpoints, per-card undo, token-protected HTTP and an output-path allow list are features none of the incumbents list together.
- Deterministic replay as a product feature, not only a test tool: bug reports as replay files, regression suites that pin a hash per tick, bit-identical exported builds, and a base for rollback networking. No engine in the set offers cross-platform determinism by default; teams buy it separately.
- Iteration speed as a measured claim. Unity users wait 15 seconds or more per script change, Unreal users report hour-scale recompiles, Bevy users fight compile times. Sub-second rebuild with state kept is worth publishing as a number next to theirs.
- Text files that merge. Unity scene merges are reported unreliable, Unreal assets are binary, GameMaker tells agents not to touch its project files. Canonical, byte-stable text scenes and prefabs help both version control and agents; show a clean three-way merge in the docs.
- Safe mods and user content. Godot's docs call mod loading a vulnerability and offer no sandbox. A sandboxed, fuel-limited game-code boundary lets a game load third-party logic without trusting it; that is rare and matches the trend toward user-generated content.
- Docs that cannot drift. The loudest documentation complaint everywhere is version mismatch. Reference pages generated from the schema, recipes executed in CI and docs served by the running engine to both people and agents remove the outdated-tutorial problem and the outdated-training-data problem at once.
- Honest hybrid positioning. Over half of surveyed developers see generative AI as harmful, Godot banned agent contributions, GameMaker made its integration opt-in, and Epic took criticism for its AI push. An engine that is fully usable by hand, records human and agent changes in one history and never requires an account or credits can win both camps; lead with determinism, testing and speed, not with 'AI builds your game'.
- Trust through licence. Permissive open source with no royalty, no seat price, no credits and no store commission contrasts with Unity's annual price rises and credit metering and with console and enterprise gates elsewhere.
- A curated package index with provenance. Fab shows what an unmoderated store becomes. Content hashes, a lock file, declared AI provenance and reviews before paid content opens would be a visible difference.
- Steam Deck and Linux on day one. 28% of surveyed developers target the Deck and Godot's only official 'console' is the Deck. A Rust engine on Vulkan can ship a verified-ready Linux export cheaply and say so.
- Publish an open agent benchmark for the engine and run it on rivals. No incumbent publishes task pass rates for its agent surface; a public, repeatable suite with results would set the terms of comparison.
- Stability promise at 1.0. Bevy's churn is its most cited cost after the missing editor. Frozen file formats with an automatic migration command and a written compatibility policy would be a differentiator among newer engines.
- Web export is the one large table-stakes gap worth sizing honestly: it is where jams, portals and sharing happen, Defold wins on a 1 MB runtime and Godot cannot export C# there. A small wasm/WebGPU-or-WebGL player would be a strong 1.0 or 1.1 item, but it grows the 1.0 scope.

### Lessons
- A new engine is judged on the boring list before anything novel: desktop export that just works, gamepad support, glTF and Blender import, tilemaps, particles, animation blending, UI, audio, a debugger and profiler, a first-game tutorial and running samples. Missing any of these reads as 'not ready' regardless of AI features.
- Do not chase Unreal on fidelity. Even Godot's 2026 releases stress correctness and predictability over new effects. A solid modern baseline (PBR, shadows on all light types, probes or baked GI, SSR, bloom, AO, AA, fog) plus a clear stylised-look story is enough; streaming open worlds and real-time GI are post-1.0.
- An open-source engine cannot ship console ports itself; Godot and Defold both route through NDA-bound partners. Say this plainly in the docs and name the path rather than leaving it unanswered.
- The editor is not optional. Bevy is five years in without one and it is the first thing every review mentions. Having an editor is an advantage only if it is polished; the 2026 Godot releases spent most of their effort on docks, themes, snapping and inspector quality-of-life.
- Breaking changes cost the ecosystem more than the core team sees: plugins lag and users stall. Freeze formats and public APIs at 1.0 and ship migrations.
- Both big vendors moved from 'MCP server' to 'CLI plus MCP' within months, and Unity reports almost all CLI use is by agents. Parity between the command line, MCP and the interface is the right architecture; keep structured output and exit codes strict.
- Charging for or metering the agent surface causes immediate backlash (Unity). Keep it free and local.
- An agent surface is an attack surface: a CVE in a community server, token-gated eval in Unity, unfinished hardening in Epic's plugin. Security features are worth documenting as features.
- Agents are weakest at art, presentation and content depth, not mechanics. Starter templates, placeholder asset creation, view description and golden images matter more for perceived quality than more editing tools.
- Silent failure kills automation: an engine that exits 0 after a script error or reports success on a no-op makes agents and CI lie. Every failure needs a non-zero result, a code and a hint.
- Tutorials age faster than engines. Tie learning material to the version and test it in CI, and keep one obvious starting path.
- Asset stores fill with low-effort AI content unless provenance and moderation exist before paid listings open.
- For the web, the compatible default is single-threaded; Godot learned this in 4.3 after years of portal and iOS trouble.
- Developer sentiment toward generative AI is negative and worsening; any AI-first message needs the hand-made path shown next to it, and the opt-in nature stated up front.

Bevy has the Bevy Remote Protocol and a community MCP server whose own listing says agents use trial and error on JSON formats and it is 'not 100% reliable'. (https://defold.com/manuals/ai-agents https://mcpservers.org/en/servers/natepiano/bevy_brp_mcp)
- [AI: benchmarks show the gap is visual and runtime verification] GameDevBench (2026, real Godot 4 projects): best result 54.5%, and adding visual feedback lifted one model from 33.3% to 47.7%. V-GameGym: code scores 70 to 90+, but screenshot and gameplay-video scores 0 to 20. GameCraft-Bench (arXiv 2606.17861, June 2026, 140 Godot tasks): best agent 41.46%, with 55% on core mechanics but 37% on art and presentation; projects that fail to launch score zero. First two from an opened blog summary, third from a search summary. (https://dev.to/abagames/is-game-programming-actually-harder-for-ai-agents-4hh1 https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17861)
- [AI: automated playtesting is fragile in existing engines] Tests on Godot 4.7.2 (Nov 2026, by a vendor of a Godot AI agent; opened): simulated clicks do not press buttons under --headless, --write-movie crashes headless, screenshots need a display, Godot exits 0 after a script error, frame-count waits are flaky without --fixed-fps. Godot has no built-in test framework (GUT and GdUnit4 are add-ons); Unity's is built in but CI needs licence activation. (https://ziva.sh/blogs/godot-automated-playtesting https://helpmetest.com/blog/godot-vs-unity-testing/)
- [AI: what practitioners say works] A May 2026 studio-tools article (vendor, opened) says agents succeed when game logic compiles and tests outside the editor, a single command runs build, tests and lint, error messages carry fix instructions, and docs are reachable by tools; they fail on visual editors, slow builds and undocumented knowledge. (https://www.metaplay.io/blog/agentic-ai-game-development-what-works-2026)
- [Determinism] A Godot community answer (2022, old) says the built-in physics is not deterministic across machines; a netcode write-up says most of Unity's built-in API including physics is not deterministic, so teams re-implement with fixed point; deterministic simulation is sold as a separate product (Photon Quantum). No source found claiming cross-platform determinism by default in any of the six engines. Search summary; Unreal not verified. (https://yal.cc/preparing-your-game-for-deterministic-netcode/ https://www.photonengine.com/quantum)
- [Pricing and trust] Unity raised Pro and Enterprise prices 5% from 12 Jan 2026 (Pro $2,310 per seat per year) and says it will adjust annually; the Runtime Fee was cancelled in 2024; a trade headline reports fresh backlash over an enterprise minimum-commitment programme. Search summary. (https://unity.com/products/pricing-updates https://mobilegamer.biz/unity-risks-fresh-backlash-with-new-fee-demands-this-feels-like-blackmail/)
- [Accessibility] Unity 6.3 extended its native screen-reader module to Windows and macOS, Unreal documents built-in screen-reader support, and Godot 4.7 added screen-reader landmark navigation in the editor. Claims that console platforms now require accessibility certification come from one blog and are unconfirmed. Search summary. (https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.3/manual/accessibility/module-intro.md https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/supporting-screen-readers-in-unreal-engine)

## Web research: launch

### Table stakes
- **Release definition and stability**: A written release policy: version meaning, what counts as a breaking change, support window per version; A list of stable vs. experimental surfaces at 1.0; Release candidates before the stable release, with a feature freeze; A migration guide and a project converter for every release that changes formats or APIs; A known-issues and what-is-missing section in the announcement; A fast first patch release planned for the weeks after launch
- **Showcase and demos**: At least one finished, playable game made with the engine, with full sources; Several small template or sample projects covering 2D, 3D and UI; Web-playable builds where possible (jam data: far more players); A showcase page with clear entry criteria for community games; Screenshots and editor captures in high resolution for press
- **Website and documentation**: A landing page that states what it is, who it is for, license and price in the first screen; Download buttons for each platform with a working installer or portable package; Getting-started path: introduction, step by step, first 2D game, first 3D game; Reference docs generated from the code and kept in sync with the release; A manual or book that covers every shipped feature at 1.0; Docs versioned per release, with outdated pages marked
- **Video**: A short release trailer or showreel (1 to 3 minutes); Short clips per headline feature embedded in the release post; A first-project walkthrough video for new users
- **Community**: One official chat space and one searchable space (forum or GitHub Discussions); A code of conduct that applies to every official space, with a report address; A game jam on itch.io soon after launch, then on a regular rhythm; A recurring ritual with the developers (live coding, office hours, dev log); Clear split between bug reports and feature proposals
- **Roadmap and communication**: A public priorities page (dated or not) and a stated release cadence; A release blog post per version with contributor credits; A news feed (RSS, social accounts) and a changelog
- **Governance and contribution**: CONTRIBUTING guide, review process and who can merge; Good-first-issue labels and a newcomer path; A stated policy on AI-assisted contributions and disclosure; Named maintainers and a plan to delegate reviews; Public statement of who owns the project assets (legal entity or individual) and how decisions are made
- **Licensing and trademark**: Permissive, standard license named precisely everywhere; no fees or royalties stated plainly; Third-party license list shipped with the engine and with exported games; A short trademark and logo policy allowing 'made with' and 'made for' uses; Press kit with logos (SVG and PNG), naming rules and a contact; Name and domain checked and secured before the announcement
- **Press and platforms**: GitHub repository public with releases, tags and release notes; Engine listed on itch.io; Steam listing for the tool itself is common for mature engines; One long announcement post suitable for Hacker News, Reddit and game-dev news sites; Direct outreach to engine-focused outlets and video channels with the press kit; Compliance note for Steam's AI disclosure rules for games made with AI-generated content
- **Funding and sustainability**: A donation or sponsorship page live at launch; A stated sustainability model (donations, sponsors, services) in the announcement; Transparency on how money is used once it is received

### Opportunities
- Define in writing what 1.0 promises and what it does not: which surfaces are stable (scene/prefab/asset file formats, the tool/MCP API, the game SDK ABI, the CLI), which are experimental, and how long a 1.x project is guaranteed to open. Godot and Rust publish such policies; Zed and Fyrox shipped 1.0 without one, and Bevy still has none. A deterministic engine with canonical files can go further than any of them and back the promise with tests (old projects and recorded replays that must still load and match in CI).
- Ship a migration tool and a migration guide with every release from day one, and never drop old-format loading without a converter. Godot's 3-to-4 converter left much manual work; Fyrox dropped asset compatibility at 1.0 with an editor converter; Bevy ships a migration guide per release but teams still left over churn.
- Treat documentation for AI agents as a first-class product. A studio left a fast-moving Rust engine partly because AI assistants' knowledge lagged the API. An engine whose docs, schemas and recipes are served to the agent at runtime from the engine itself removes that lag; say so explicitly at launch and show a measured task success rate with the method published.
- Position as hybrid, not AI-only. 52% of surveyed developers see generative AI as harmful, an AI-first editor had to add a full AI off switch, and Godot banned AI-authored contributions. A launch message of 'fully usable by hand, every agent action reviewable and undoable, AI can be switched off' addresses the hostile half of the audience without losing the other.
- Make ownership and export the headline contrast with prompt-to-game products: plain text project files on disk, a real exported game, permissive license, no credits, no lock-in, bring your own agent. The recurring criticism of funded AI game tools is lock-in, no export and credit caps.
- Replace 'prompt a full game' claims with verifiable ones: reproducible runs, scenario tests, replays that match bit for bit, published evaluation transcripts and a side-by-side comparison with a mainstream engine on the same tasks. The big-vendor promise was judged 'a scaffolding tool, not a design tool'; evidence-first messaging is unoccupied ground.
- Launch with small finished games that people can play in a browser or download in one click, with full project sources, plus a short showreel. Godot keeps a curated showcase and annual showreel; Blender proved 2.8 with a production film; Fyrox's 1.0 post had neither games nor video and got little reach. Jam data says web builds get far more players.
- Run a launch jam on itch.io a few weeks after 1.0, with a declared AI rule (for example two tracks: by hand, and with an agent). Bevy's first jam drew 73 entries and jams are now the most visible adoption metric for engines (GMTK share). A jam also works as a structured first-run test, as O3DE did with a diary-study jam.
- Publish a priorities page without dates instead of a dated roadmap, plus a clear post-1.0 cadence (for example a patch within weeks, minors on a fixed rhythm with a feature window and a bugfix window). Godot adopted exactly this after its long 4.0 cycle and avoids dated roadmaps to not over-promise.
- Set up the organizational basics before launch rather than years after: who owns the name, logo, domain and accounts; a short trademark and logo policy that allows 'made with' use; a press kit; a code of conduct; a contribution guide with an explicit AI-contribution rule and a route for newcomers. Bevy and Godot both formalized these late and called it debt.
- Measure and publish time-to-first-playable for a new user on a clean machine (installer to running template game), and fix whatever the first three outside testers hit. O3DE cut first-run from hours to minutes after user research; Godot's adopters cite a lightweight editor and fast iteration as the main draw.
- Get the license wording exactly right in every channel and state plainly that there are no fees, royalties or store commission. Defold had to walk back 'open source'; trust shocks (Unity fee, The Machinery EULA) moved users and money to permissively licensed engines.
- Choose one honest definition of 'stable' for the announcement and name who should wait. Godot told stability-minded users to stay on the old version; Zed said '1.0 doesn't mean done'. For a brand-new engine with no user base, a candid 'what is strong, what is thin, what is missing' section builds more trust than a feature list.
- Plan the announcement as one long post that carries everything (goals, each capability with a clip or image, the showcase games, benchmarks with method, what is missing, what is next, how to help), submitted to Hacker News and the language community forum, with the engine also listed free on itch.io and Steam. This is the format that put a first engine release at the top of those sites.
- Put a funding ask and a sustainability statement in the launch itself. Godot found donations do not rise with users unless asked; Blender's fund nearly doubled around its landmark release because a campaign was running; Zed stated its business model in the open-source post.

### Lessons
- State what 'stable' means before release and tell cautious users to wait; candor at launch (Godot 4.0, Bevy 0.1) was received better than polish claims.
- Do not promise dates or features you do not control: Godot 3.0's 'within a few weeks' and Blender 2.8's slipping schedule became the story; Godot now refuses dated roadmaps.
- A long rewrite before a big release is costly; after it, switch to short fixed cycles and never delay for one more feature.
- Breaking changes are the main reason teams leave young engines. If you must break, ship a converter, a migration guide and release candidates so the ecosystem can catch up.
- A 1.0 without a written compatibility policy is only a version number; with one, it is the reason to adopt.
- Docs must ship with the release, not after; assume every third-party tutorial and every AI model's memory of your API is out of date the day you change it.
- Showcase games and a video carry a launch; a feature list alone does not travel beyond existing followers.
- First-run experience decides adoption: hours to first project drives people away, minutes keeps them.
- Use precise license language. Calling a restricted license 'open source' forces a public correction.
- Trust is the asset open engines win on: no fees, no retroactive terms, user-owned files. Every proprietary engine crisis moved users toward permissive licenses.
- Delegate early: a single reviewer or owner becomes the bottleneck and the bus factor within months of a successful launch.
- Formalize ownership of name, logo, domain and money early; fixing it later is described by projects as organizational debt.
- A trademark policy that restricts ordinary community use causes backlash; allow 'made with' and 'made for' uses and consult before publishing.
- Funding has to be asked for and tied to a visible moment (release, campaign); user growth alone does not raise it.
- AI is a divisive label in games: over half of developers view generative AI negatively, so offer AI as optional and controllable, and keep an off switch.
- Prompt-to-game claims are widely doubted; assessments describe them as scaffolding. Claim what can be reproduced and show the evidence.
- Decide your policy on AI-generated contributions before opening the repository; maintainers elsewhere were overwhelmed by low-effort AI pull requests.
- Rest after 1.0 is real: small teams announce a pause right after release, so line up the first patch release and community replies in advance.

### Findings (with sources)
- [Godot 4.0 launch (March 2023): honest framing] The 4.0 announcement (1 March 2023, after 3+ years, 17 alphas, 17 betas, 6 RCs) told users to expect "workflow-breaking bugs (especially on less common hardware)", unpolished workflows and unoptimized performance, flagged GDExtension and navigation as experimental, and said docs for some areas lagged. It kept 3.x alive as an LTS line (3.6) so nobody was forced to migrate. Secondary coverage gives "4 years / 12,000 merged PRs"; the post itself says "3+ years". (https://godotengine.org/article/godot-4-0-sets-sail/)
- [Godot 4.0: definition of 'stable' and who should wait] Three months before launch the team published its release criteria: "We will release 4.0 when we believe it is ready to be used in production, not when it is perfect" and "4.0 is only the beginning of Godot 4". It explicitly told users who want stability to stay on 3.5, entered feature freeze in August 2022, ran weekly betas, and promised fast 4.0.x bugfix releases. The same post admits 3.0 shipped with major gaps (no GLES2, no C# export, many bugs). (https://godotengine.org/article/release-management-4-0-and-beyond/)
- [Godot after 4.0: fixed cadence instead of big-bang releases] After the long 4.0 cycle the project moved to roughly four-month minor releases (4.1 came 4 months after 4.0 and was pitched as stability/performance/polish), with a fixed feature window, a bugfix-only window, and a rule not to delay a release for "just one more feature". Community threads at the time still called 4.0 not production ready and advised waiting for 4.1 (anecdotal forum posts). (https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/about/release_policy.html ; https://godotengine.org/article/release-management-4-0-and-beyond/)
- [Godot: written release and compatibility policy] Godot documents a semver-like policy: major = compatibility breaks needing porting; minor = features with only narrow breakage; patch = backwards compatible. Renaming/removing a method, member or class, or changing a default that affects existing projects, is reserved for a major release. A support table states which versions get full, partial or no support. (https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/about/release_policy.html)
- [Godot 3 to 4 migration cost] The official upgrade guide says the converter "cannot cater to all situations", a large part of the upgrade is manual, scripts may have hundreds of errors, the tool makes no backup, and some features were lost in the rewrite. This is the documented cost of a breaking major version even with a converter. (https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.4/tutorials/migrating/upgrading_to_godot_4.html)
- [Godot 3.0 launch (January 2018): docs shipped with the release, promises slipped] 3.0 (29 Jan 2018, ~18 months of work) shipped with brand-new built-in and online docs written with known tutorial authors, but noted existing tutorials "will need to be remade for 3.x". It deferred GLES2, WebGL 1 and the visual shader editor to 3.1 and promised C# export in 3.0.1 "within a few weeks" (a forward promise). It broke compatibility with 2.1. Funding came from Patreon plus Mozilla and Microsoft grants. Source is 2018 and old. (https://godotengine.org/article/godot-3-0-released)
- [Godot: no dated roadmap, a priorities page instead] The Godot Foundation says "We have avoided providing a roadmap" because "we don't want to promise more than we can deliver" and to avoid pressure on volunteers; it publishes a priorities page (largest tasks, no dates). In June 2026 it added a written vision: free with no paid tiers, best for small-to-medium teams, a smaller solid engine over a large fragile one, features that work "reliably and completely", good on low-end hardware. (https://godotengine.org/article/godot-foundation-update-dec-2024/ ; https://godot.foundation/policies-and-procedures/project-vision-statement)
- [Godot: trademark policy and press kit] The Godot Foundation owns and has registered the marks (EU, USA) and publishes a trademark policy (version 21 July 2025): "made with" and "made for" uses are allowed without permission; using the name for a commercial engine or fork, in a company name, or the logo as your own logo is not. The press kit offers logos in SVG/PNG under CC BY 4.0 with simple usage rules and naming guidance. (https://godot.foundation/policies-and-procedures/trademark-policy ; https://godotengine.org/press/)
- [Godot: showcase and showreel as standing launch assets] Godot runs a curated showcase (desktop games need about 25 Steam reviews; selection otherwise subjective) and an annual community-submitted showreel video with categories for desktop, mobile and tools (2026 submissions closed 1 October 2026). The 4.0 announcement itself highlighted open-source demo projects and a shipped game's port. (https://godotengine.org/article/new-showcase-for-projects-made-with-godot ; https://showreel.godotengine.org/about)
- [Godot: distribution and community spaces] The engine itself is listed free on Steam and on itch.io (itch devlog mirrors release posts). The community page separates official spaces (forum, Discord, Reddit, social, contributors chat, GitHub with a separate proposals repo) from community-run ones, and requires every listed space to follow the Code of Conduct. (https://store.steampowered.com/app/404790 ; https://godotengine.itch.io/godot ; https://godotengine.org/community/)
- [Godot adoption evidence: jams and a trust shock] Godot's share of the GMTK Game Jam rose from about 13-16% (2021-22) to 37% (2024), 39% (2025, 9,724 entries) and 47% in 2026, passing Unity for the first time; older-year figures differ slightly between sources. Game Developer (Aug 2026) attributes the rise to the 2023 Unity runtime fee fallout plus a lightweight editor and fast iteration; developers still cite documentation gaps. 12% of surveyed developers name it their engine of choice. (https://gameworldobserver.com/2026/07/29/unity-is-no-longer-the-leader-godot-has-become-the-most-popular-engine-at-gmtk-game-jam-2026 ; https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/godot-adoption-is-rising-what-are-devs-enjoying-about-the-engine-)
- [Godot: funding does not follow users automatically] Godot's own 2026 growth post says downloads reach about 2 million per release and the community doubled in two years, yet donations have not risen with user growth; the author concludes that asking for donations is "the best way to increase donations". The Development Fund launched in September 2023. (https://godotengine.org/article/godot-growth-stats-2026/)
- [Godot: AI contribution policy (June 2026)] Godot announced that autonomous AI agents and "vibe coding" PRs lead to a ban, substantial AI-generated code is not accepted, any AI use must be disclosed, AI-written text in issues/PRs is banned, and newcomers (3 or fewer merged PRs) need maintainer permission before features or refactors. Reasoning: reviewer capacity did not grow with PR volume and "AI cannot take responsibility". The policy covers contributions to the engine, not what users build with it. (https://godotengine.org/article/contribution-policy-2026/)
- [Bevy 0.1 announcement (August 2020): one long, show-everything post] The first Bevy post was a single long article: stated design goals, a section per feature with code samples, screenshots, ECS benchmark charts, an example Breakout game, a 'what's next' focus list, and calls to action (quick start guide, GitHub, Discord, donate). It warned plainly that the engine was in a prototyping phase, "APIs will change", and it was not recommended for serious projects. (https://bevy.org/news/introducing-bevy/)
- [Bevy: first-week and first-year results, and the author's own lessons] Self-reported: within a week the post was #2 on Hacker News and the third most popular r/rust post ever, with 2,200 stars and 26 new contributors; after a year 10,030 stars, 255 contributors, 4,871 Discord members. The creator's stated mistakes: he "waited way too long" to delegate reviews (PR backlog), announced focus areas did not match the work done, an early renderer API was a costly mistake, and rapid rewrites frustrated users. (https://bevy.org/news/bevys-first-birthday/)
- [Bevy: release process] Bevy releases roughly every three to four months with no fixed dates ("to avoid crunch"), publishes weekly release candidates so ecosystem crates can update, and every release ships a blog post, a migration guide, a change log, a contributor list and an updated book. It is still on 0.x versions (a 0.19 to 0.20 migration guide exists), i.e. no 1.0 or stability promise yet. (https://bevy.org/learn/contribute/project-information/release-process/ ; https://bevy.org/learn/migration-guides/0-19-to-0-20/)
- [Bevy: churn drives a real team away; AI knowledge lag named as a reason] A studio that built on Bevy from December 2023 ported to Unity in early 2025 (six-week port). Reasons given: frequent API changes and regressions, steep learning curve for a new programmer, no clear scripting/modding path, and that because the engine changes fast "AI knowledge lags behind", cutting the benefit of AI assistants. They praised the ECS and the community. Reddit commenters disagreed on whether the engine or the language was the cause. (https://hn.jakechampion.name/reader/https://deadmoney.gg/news/articles/migrating-away-from-rust)
- [Bevy: governance and funding formalized late] The Bevy Foundation (March 2024, later a US 501(c)(3)) was created to pay down "organizational debt": donations had gone to one person's sponsor page, one person held the logo copyright, domain and infrastructure (bus factor), and the project lacked a legal entity for larger donors. Board members must be active maintainers; bylaws, minutes and budget are public. No trademark policy was found in the sources. (https://bevy.org/news/bevy-foundation/)
- [Bevy: jams and learning material] The first official Bevy Jam (Feb-Mar 2022, on itch.io) drew 73 entries and 1,618 ratings; the sixth (2025) drew 98. The jam page notes entries with a web build get many more players. Community learning resources went stale as the API moved: the popular unofficial cheatbook now says it is outdated and unmaintained (community commentary, mostly 2021-2024). (https://itch.io/jam/bevy-jam-1 ; https://itch.io/jam/bevy-jam-5 ; https://bevy-cheatbook.github.io/tutorial/guide.html)
- [Defold going source-available (May 2020): the wording mistake] King moved Defold to an independent foundation and published the source under an Apache-2.0-derived license with a clause that forbids commercializing the engine itself. It was first announced as open source; after pushback the team changed its wording and now calls it a "source available game engine" with a "developer-friendly license", explaining the OSI definition requires allowing commercialization. Early press coverage mislabeled it as plain Apache. (https://forum.defold.com/t/we-are-finally-sharing-the-defold-source-code/65177 ; https://defold.com/open)
- [Defold: promises backed by structure, and how it is funded] Defold states it will not move to subscriptions, royalties or license fees, and says the foundation's objectives are protected by Swedish foundation law. Funding is mainly corporate partnerships (which pay most salaries, per the foundation) plus donations via Patreon/PayPal/GitHub Sponsors; it started with a large founding donation and two full-time people. The product owner described pre-release work: reviewing every commit for keys and certificates, and open-sourcing small modules first to test the waters. Interview is from 2020. (https://defold.com/open ; https://defold.com/2020/06/05/Interview-with-Defold-product-owner/ ; https://defold.com/donate)
- [Zed going open source (January 2024)] Zed open-sourced everything at once with a clear license split and reasoning (GPL editor, AGPL server, Apache-2.0 UI framework), a stated business model (paid services, proprietary code to stay "a tiny fraction"), a contribution guide, a public roadmap built from user feedback, and a community ritual launched the next day ("Fireside Hacks": the team coding live with whoever shows up). (https://zed.dev/blog/zed-is-now-open-source)
- [Zed 1.0 (April 2026): what 1.0 was defined to mean] The 1.0 post says "1.0 doesn't mean 'done'"; it means most developers can quickly feel at home. It came after about five years and over a thousand shipped versions, on macOS, Windows and Linux, with weekly releases continuing. The post states no formal stability guarantee; third-party write-ups claiming stable extension APIs and a durable settings format are not confirmed by the post. Sources give 29 April or 1 May 2026 as the date. (https://zed.dev/blog/zed-1-0)
- [Zed: an AI-first tool had to add a full AI off switch] In July 2025 Zed, marketed as an AI code editor, added a global setting to disable all AI features and promised an onboarding switch, after GitHub requests from people who could not or would not use AI. Coverage called it ironic; by 1.0 the AI features were described as optional. (https://zed.dev/blog/disable-ai-features)
- [Blender 2.80 (July 2019): funded sprint, dogfooding and convention changes] 2.80 shipped at SIGGRAPH 2019 after about three years. The 'Code Quest' crowdfunding (USB 'rocket' tokens, target hit in 4 days, almost 100K USD raised) funded a focused sprint to reach beta. The open movie 'Spring' was produced on development builds to give 2.8 "a full production test" before release. The UI adopted industry conventions (dark theme, left-click select), which most coverage praised and some veterans disliked. (https://www.blender.org/2-8/quest ; https://studio.blender.org/films/spring/pages/about/ ; https://www.cgchannel.com/?p=106554)
- [Blender 2.80: schedule slip and funding effect] Developers warned in 2017 that they "kept adding project targets" for 2.8; a user-reported original target of October 2018 slipped about ten months to July 2019 (the slip is confirmed, the exact original date is from a forum comment). Around the release the Development Fund nearly doubled within months (from 22.4k EUR/month at the start of 2019 to about 37.5k EUR/month), Epic gave a 1.2M USD grant, and a magazine reported funding passing 1M USD/year. Sources are 2018-2019. (https://code.blender.org/2019/07/development-fund-report-july-2019/ ; https://lists.blender.org/pipermail/bf-committers/2017-November/048910.html ; https://www.cgchannel.com/?p=105775)
- [Fyrox 1.0 (March 2026): a small-team 1.0 as consolidation] The Rust engine Fyrox reached 1.0 after seven years with "little to no funding" and a small team. 1.0 was mostly consolidation after two release candidates: the book finished ("pretty much every chapter"), a CLI for CI export, and an editor tool to convert old assets because 1.0 dropped old-format compatibility. The announcement contains no showcase games, no video and no stated stability promise, and says major features pause for months so the team can rest. Coverage was limited to niche outlets. (https://fyrox.rs/blog/post/fyrox-game-engine-1-0-0/ ; https://fyrox.rs/blog/post/fyrox-game-engine-1-0-0-rc-1/ ; https://gamefromscratch.com/after-7-years-of-development-fyrox-1-0-is-here/)
- [Stability promise: the Rust 1.0 model] Rust framed 1.0 as "a commitment to stability": you should "never dread upgrading". The mechanism was explicit: stable/beta/nightly channels, unstable features gated and opt-in only, a six-week train, and a written list of what is excluded from the promise. The authors argue that labeling things unstable is not enough; the stable channel must actually exclude them. Source is 2014 but is the standard reference. (https://blog.rust-lang.org/2014/10/30/Stability/)
- [First-run experience: O3DE] O3DE's own UX team reported that its predecessor's first-time setup took almost four hours and drove new users away; research with 40+ users and a month-long intern game jam (diary study) led to a redesign claiming under ten minutes. At its 2021 launch the Linux Foundation engine was criticized for being Windows-only. No independent adoption figures were found; the project's 2024 notes name visibility as a problem. (https://docs.o3de.org/blog/posts/o3de-ftue-blog-post-1/ ; https://lunduke.substack.com/p/the-linux-foundations-3d-game-engine)
- [Trust and licensing failures] The Machinery engine was cancelled in 2022 and users were told to delete source and binaries under a EULA clause reportedly added after the fact; commentators called it the best advertisement for open source engines. Unity's 2023 per-install fee led studios to redirect money to open engines (one donated 100K USD each to two projects) and to say trust would not return after a walk-back. (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/developer-our-machinery-is-ending-its-game-engine-and-asks-users-to-destroy-source-code ; https://www.pcgamer.com/terraria-developer-sticks-it-to-unity-with-dollar200k-donation-to-open-source-competitors-doesnt-even-use-unity-itself)
- [Trademark policy can backfire] The Rust Foundation's 2023 draft trademark policy restricted ordinary community uses (for example the name in tool or crate names) and caused a backlash; the foundation apologized for the process and published a simpler draft in November 2024. Lesson drawn by the project: a trademark policy must reflect community priorities and be consulted on openly. (https://blog.rust-lang.org/inside-rust/2023/04/12/trademark-policy-draft-feedback/ ; https://blog.rust-lang.org/2024/11/06/trademark-update/)
- [AI sentiment among developers (2026)] The GDC 2026 State of the Game Industry survey (2,300+ respondents) found 52% say generative AI is harming the industry (30% in 2025, 18% the year before) and 7% positive; 36% use generative AI at work, mostly for research and code help. One outlet headlines 55% instead of 52%. A separate Omdia survey reported use falling to 29%. (https://gdconf.com/article/gdc-2026-state-of-the-game-industry-reveals-impact-of-layoffs-generative-ai-and-more/ ; https://www.techspot.com/news/111128-more-than-50-game-developers-generative-ai-harming.html)
- [AI sentiment among players and store rules (2026)] Valve narrowed Steam's AI disclosure in January 2026: AI-assisted development tools (code assistants) need no disclosure, but pre-generated and live-generated content that players consume must be declared. A mid-2026 survey of about 3,800 Steam users reportedly found 43% fine with AI in games and 8% who would not buy, with low trust in honest disclosure. Claims that AI labels hurt sales are unverified. All secondary coverage, not Valve's own text. (https://games.slashdot.org/story/26/01/19/1735231/valve-has-significantly-rewritten-steams-rules-for-how-developers-must-disclose-ai-use ; https://games.gg/news/steam-players-ai-games-survey/)
- [AI game creation: big-vendor promises vs. assessment] Unity's CEO said in February 2026 a beta would let people "prompt full casual games into existence"; it drew pre-show skepticism, and a GDC recap (by a vendor of MCP tools) judged the result "a scaffolding tool, not a design tool" whose quality drops for unusual mechanics. Unity AI reached open beta in May 2026 with the note that features "may change, be limited, or be discontinued without notice". No independent hands-on review was found. (https://www.pcgamer.com/software/ai/unity-boss-who-once-called-out-the-idiocy-of-the-metaverse-now-says-his-companys-new-ai-tech-will-enable-developers-to-prompt-full-casual-games-into-existence-with-natural-language-only ; https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/gdc-2026-ai-takeaways-indie-developers)
- [AI game creation: funded startups and their weak points] 2025-2026 saw many funded entrants: Astrocade (56M USD), Moonlake (about 30M USD, beta in 2026), Sett (27M), SPARQ (8.5M seed round), Jabali (5M), plus browser tools such as Rosebud. Coverage is mostly funding news. Recurring criticisms (largely from competing vendors, so biased): browser tools keep games on their own site with limited or no code export, credit caps, and unclear pricing and export formats. One analyst piece doubts AI-made games find an audience outside UGC platforms. (https://pocketgamer.biz/astrocade-raises-56m-to-expand-ai-powered-games-creation-platform/ ; https://app.cinevva.com/signals/2026-03-04-everyone-wants-ai-game-engine ; https://omdia.tech.informa.com/om148254/aigenerated-games-are-coming-but-will-anyone-play-them)
- [MCP is becoming standard in incumbent engines, but as an add-on] Unity describes an official MCP server inside its AI tools package (June 2026 post; one third-party guide says Unity's docs now mark it deprecated in favor of a CLI, which conflicts). Unreal Engine 5.8 (June 2026) added an experimental MCP plugin; forum users report dropped connections. Godot has only third-party MCP plugins. GDC 2026 held a first MCP showcase with about 40 game servers (vendor report). (https://unity.com/blog/mcp-servers-game-development ; https://www.unrealengine.com/news/state-of-unreal-2026-top-news-from-the-show ; https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/5-8-experimental-modelcontextprotocol-mcp-server-instantly-drops-connections/2729488)
- [Other new 'AI-native' open engines exist] A March 2026 vendor article lists new agent-first engines: one open-source Rust + WebGPU engine with YAML scenes, an MCP command interface and headless rendering for automated tests; one Apache-2.0 code-first 2D engine with a Rust core and TypeScript scripting; and others. The author (a competitor) predicts "Most of these engines won't survive" but that the patterns (text scenes, MCP control) will be absorbed by mainstream engines in about two years. No independent reception data found. (https://app.cinevva.com/signals/2026-03-24-ai-native-engines-are-shipping)
- [AI-built games as community events] The 2025 'Vibe Jam' (rule: at least 80% of the game written by AI) drew 1,000+ entries (one report says 1,170); coverage is enthusiastic but largely promotional, and no independent quality review was found. Godot's monthly community jam, by contrast, now bans AI-generated art, music and writing. Jam rules on AI are therefore a positioning choice. (https://vibej.am/2025 ; https://itch.io/jam/godot-wild-jam-90)

### Caveats
Scope and method: web research only on 18 November 2026; no project or personal names were used in queries. Most Godot, Bevy, Zed, Defold, Fyrox and Rust facts come from the projects' own posts, which are primary but self-reported (for example Bevy's first-week Hacker News and Reddit rankings and star counts are the author's own figures).

Pages that could not be read directly: the Blender 2.80 release page and a Fast Company hands-on article on an AI game tool returned HTTP 403, and a guessed Godot 4.0 release page returned 404; facts for those topics come from other pages or secondary coverage. The studio post about leaving Bevy was read through a mirror, not the original site.

Old sources: Godot 3.0 (2018), Blender 2.8 material (2017-2019), Defold interview (2020), Rust stability post (2014), O3DE first-run post (2022), Bevy first-birthday post (2021). They are still the standard references for those launches but describe conditions at the time.

Disagreements between sources: Godot 4.0 development length ('3+ years' in the post vs. '4 years' in secondary coverage) and which version was called LTS (the post says 3.6); Zed 1.0 date (29 April vs. 1 May 2026) and whether 1.0 carries API stability commitments (the official post states none; third-party posts claim some); Unreal 5.8 release date (17 vs. 23 June 2026); GDC survey negative share (52% in most outlets, 55% in one headline) and AI usage (36% vs. 40%; a separate survey says 29%); whether Unity's official MCP server is current or deprecated in favor of a CLI; early-year GMTK engine shares; whether Godot's rule bans all or only substantial AI-generated code (the official post: substantial code banned, minor disclosed use allowed); the original Blender 2.8 target date (from a forum comment); Defold's label ('open source' in early press vs. 'source available' by the foundation).

Weak or biased evidence: almost all commentary on AI game creation tools (lock-in, export, pricing, 'won't survive' predictions, GDC recaps, MCP ecosystem counts) comes from vendors that sell competing products, or from funding press releases; no independent hands-on reviews of prompt-to-game products were found, and reception of the new agent-first open engines could not be verified (no repository, license or usage data confirmed). Player-sentiment figures and the claim that AI labels hurt Steam sales are from secondary outlets and unverified. Steam's AI rules were read from news coverage, not Valve's documentation. Community complaints about Godot 4.0 readiness and Bevy docs are anecdotal forum and Reddit posts. No independent adoption figures were found for O3DE. Bevy's exact current version was reported inconsistently (0.18 vs. 0.20); only 'still 0.x' is relied on. The Blender-on-Steam and 2.80-specific download numbers were not found.

Not covered or thin: Godot 4.0 and Blender 2.80 launch videos specifically, detailed press outreach playbooks, Epic Games Store distribution of engines, and legal specifics of trademark registration; these would need separate research before decisions that depend on them.
